# Sight lost and not given back: ideas for a six-part series

Written 20 October 2026, after the four Law episodes were posted. Nothing here is a script. These are the
options for Brian to read and decide on, in the same spirit as law_series_ideas.md.

## Brian's brief (20 October, from chat)

Back to the formula of episodes 1 to 6 (imagined scenes return). The six people on the site's page "Sight lost and
not given back": Isaac, Jacob, Eli, Ahijah, Samson, Zedekiah. His question: is there enough for six full episodes?
The pages are thin, so each needs expanding: imagined scenes, the Hebrew word where there is one, other voices (few,
since the scenes have voices), where we are, the shelf dug into more deeply, further questions answered, and his own
thoughts in detail later. Claude may suggest things he might say, for him to accept or not.

## Is there an episode in each? Yes, with two caveats

The story pages run about 300 words each, with two or three shelf items and Brian's thoughts on only two. But the
Bible text behind each is longer than the Law passages were, and the shelf is deep: Matthew Henry comments on every
verse, Josephus retells five of the six, the 1915 encyclopedia has entries on all six, and Levy's 1872 history of
blindness has whole chapters on Isaac and on Samson, written by a blind man. Imagined scenes, which the Law series
did without, add three to five minutes to an episode on their own.

Caveats: Jacob (Genesis 48) and Ahijah (1 Kings 14) are the shortest texts. Each has a strong enough turn to carry
an episode (below), but they will lean more on the scenes, the shelf and Brian than Isaac, Eli, Samson and
Zedekiah will. If either feels thin when drafted, the fallback is to pair Isaac and Jacob (the hands) and run five.

## The shape, carried over from episodes 1 to 6 and 7 to 10

Cold open (a line of the text, or a sound) -> Brian's question, with a fresh Mister Scaredy Cat line -> SERIES_INTRO
(unchanged, reusable) -> a new shared intro for this series, number-free so the take can be reused -> where we
are, with the map -> the passage, read word for word -> imagined scene, announced with "imagine" -> the old books ->
the word (kept from the Law series: it worked) -> other voices where there is one -> Brian -> teaser -> CLOSE.

Draft of the shared intro, for Brian to shape: "This episode is part of our series on sight lost and not given back:
six people in the Bible who lost their sight and lived the rest of their lives without it. Four went blind in old
age. Two were blinded by their enemies. When we imagine a scene, we will tell you so. Everything else is from the
text, from the old books, and from Brian."

Voices: NARRATOR, READER, BRIAN, HENRY (George) and BOOKS (Mihai) carry over. Scene voices only where a scene needs
one who speaks: Isaac, Jacob (one old-man voice could serve both, which is itself the point), Eli, Samson, a boy
(Samuel, and the lad who leads Samson). Rebekah and the queen could be heard but need not speak. Brian wanted few.

The word: the Hebrew is as rich here as in the Law. Three of the age stories use the same verb, kahah, "to grow dim",
of eyes (Isaac, Eli, and Moses whose eyes did NOT dim, Deuteronomy 34:7). Jacob's eyes are kabed, "heavy". Ahijah's
are qum, "set" or "fixed". Samson's are naqar, "gouged out", a word also used for digging a well. Zedekiah is
ivver, the plain word. The checker (src/hebrew.py) needs the tagged Hebrew for Genesis, Judges, 1 Samuel, 1 Kings,
Jeremiah and Ezekiel, which it does not have yet; same source as before (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible), so it needs
only Brian's yes to fetch.

Other voices: Levy's "Blindness and the Blind" (1872) is a blind author writing about Isaac and Samson. That is the
closest thing on the shelf to Brian's own seat, and it could be a recurring voice in this series, read by BOOKS.

## Order

Page order is Isaac, Jacob, Eli, Ahijah, Samson, Zedekiah: the four who went blind with age, then the two who were
blinded. Claude's recommendation: keep the four age stories first, then Zedekiah, and end on Samson, whose last
prayer "for my two eyes" is heard. It lets the series end on God hearing a blind man rather than on a king led to
Babylon in chains. Bible order (Isaac, Jacob, Samson, Eli, Ahijah, Zedekiah) is the other defensible choice.

Numbering continues: episodes 11 to 16.

## The six episodes

### 1. Isaac and the stolen blessing (Genesis 27; also 25:27-34 and 28:1-5 for before and after)

Spine: Brian's own point, already on the page. Isaac checks with every sense he has left. What beats him is not his
blindness but a family who knew exactly which senses he would use and prepared a disguise for each.
Questions to answer: how long did Isaac live after saying "I do not know the day of my death"? (Genesis 35:28: he
was 180; about 43 more years.) Why did the voice, which he got right, lose to the hands and the smell? What did
"the smell of a field the LORD has blessed" mean to a man who could not see the field?
Imagined scenes: Rebekah fitting the goatskins to Jacob's arms (seen from her side, Jacob silent and afraid); Isaac
alone after Esau's cry, the trembling of verse 33.
Scene voices: Isaac; possibly Jacob's two lines.
The word: kahah, dim (27:1), followed to Eli (1 Samuel 3:2), Moses (Deuteronomy 34:7, where it is denied), and Job
17:7. The word for "trembled violently" (charad) in 27:33 is worth a line: a very great trembling.
Shelf: Henry on Genesis 27 (long, and hard on Rebekah and Jacob); Josephus I.18 (the goatskin on the arm); the 1915
encyclopedia on cataract in old age; Levy 1872, "The Patriarch Isaac" (a blind man's reading).
Suggested things Brian might say: his page note on touch (if Isaac could not tell the hands, his touch had gone
too); what it is to be told something is so by people you must trust; whether he, like Isaac, trusts voice first.

### 2. Jacob blesses Joseph's sons (Genesis 48; and 49:1-2, 28 for the twelve)

Spine: the mirror. The man who fooled a blind father with his hands now crosses his own hands on purpose, and when
his sighted son reaches in to correct him, says "I know, my son, I know."
Questions to answer: how much could Jacob see? (48:10, heavy with age, "could not see", but 48:8 "saw Joseph's
sons", so shapes.) Did Jacob remember Genesis 27 in that moment? The text does not say; Henry thinks so; Brian
decides how far to go. Why does he bless the twelve in chapter 49 with such detail, blind?
Imagined scenes: Joseph bringing the boys to the bed and placing them right-hand to right-hand; the moment the hands
cross and Joseph's hand goes out to move his father's.
Scene voices: Jacob (same voice as Isaac?), Joseph.
The word: kabed, heavy (48:10), the word for Pharaoh's hardened heart and for the glory of God. Also "he kissed
them and embraced them": touch as recognition, the same senses as Genesis 27 used rightly.
Shelf: Henry on Genesis 48 ("the eye of faith may be clear when the eye of the body is dim"); Smith's on Goshen;
the encyclopedia on the right hand and blessing; Josephus II.8.
Suggested things Brian might say: his page note already says Jacob "proves the point by knowingly placing his
hands on the opposite sons"; the familiar moment of a sighted person reaching in to correct a blind person's
deliberate choice (the page's own line); what it is like when "I know" is not believed.

### 3. Eli at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:9-18 for the earlier Eli; 3:1-18; 4:12-18)

Spine: the man who cannot see is the one who recognizes the voice of God when the sighted boy does not. Then the
other side: at the gate, everything reaches him by sound, last of all.
Questions to answer: in chapter 1 Eli watches Hannah's mouth and misjudges her as drunk. Was his sight already
going, or is this the sighted Eli making a sighted man's mistake? How did a blind priest serve at Shiloh at all,
given the Law of episode 9? (He sat by the doorpost; the text has him judging, not at the altar.) Why "the lamp of
God had not yet gone out" (3:3): a time of night, in a house where one man could not see it.
Imagined scenes: the night in the house of the LORD, Samuel running to Eli three times (announced); the gate at
Shiloh, the runner from the battle, the outcry, Eli asking what the noise means.
Scene voices: Eli, Samuel (a boy).
The word: kahah again (3:2) and qum, "set" (4:15, "his eyes were set so that he could not see"), shared with
Ahijah. The word for the runner's "What is this uproar?" The name Ichabod, "the glory has departed", spoken over
the same hour.
Shelf: Henry on 1 Samuel 3 and 4 (he notes Eli's blindness and his sitting "by the wayside watching", a blind man
watching); Josephus V.11; Smith's on Shiloh and on Eli; the encyclopedia "Eli"; the Law pages' material on the
blemished priest for the cross-reference.
Suggested things Brian might say: hearing God when others do not; waiting for news everyone else can read on a
face; the page's line that he recognizes the voice of God "when the sighted boy does not."

### 4. Ahijah and the disguised queen (1 Kings 14:1-18; 11:29-39 for the torn cloak)

Spine: the one person in the room who cannot see well is the one who knows what is really happening, and he knows
it because God told him. The disguise was made for eyes.
Questions to answer: why disguise her at all for a man who could not see? (The page and Brian: low vision, worth a
try; and the servants on the road.) What did she carry? (loaves, cakes, honey: a poor woman's gift, part of the
disguise.) What became of the boy? (He died as she crossed the threshold, and he alone of Jeroboam's house was
mourned and buried.) How long between the torn cloak and this day?
Imagined scenes: the queen on the road from Tirzah to Shiloh in plain clothes with her basket; the doorway, the
sound of her feet, the old man speaking her name before she speaks.
Scene voices: Ahijah; the queen need not speak (she says nothing in the text either).
The word: qum, "set" (14:4), shared with Eli; and "I am sent to you with harsh news" (qasheh, hard).
Shelf: Henry on 1 Kings 14; Josephus VIII.11; Smith's on Tirzah and Shiloh; the encyclopedia "Ahijah".
Suggested things Brian might say: his page notes on footsteps (he can tell Barb's shoes, but not among others);
the difference between recognizing and being told; being underestimated by a disguise.

### 5. Zedekiah, the last king of Judah (2 Kings 25:1-7; Jeremiah 39:4-7; 52:8-11; Ezekiel 12:10-13; Jeremiah
34:1-5 and 8-22)

Spine: the order of events is the cruelty. The last thing he sees is his sons' deaths; then he is blinded so that it
stays the last thing. And two prophecies met in that act: Jeremiah said he would see the king of Babylon face to
face; Ezekiel said he would be brought to Babylon "yet he will not see it".
Questions to answer: how could both prophecies be true? (Josephus noticed; the answer is the blinding at Riblah,
before the journey.) What did Jeremiah promise him in chapter 34 (to die in peace, with burning for him as for his
fathers), and was it kept? Jeremiah 34 is also the broken release of the servants, which ties back to episode 8.
Imagined scenes: the night flight through the gate between the two walls; Riblah, the sentence, told without
showing it (seen from the side, a sound, a silence); the road north, a blind king in bronze shackles, walked past
the places he cannot see. Must be calm and never graphic, by the house rules.
Scene voices: possibly none; this may be an episode the narrator and reader carry alone, as the Law episodes did.
The word: ivver, blind (plain), and the verb "put out" (avar, to blind, in the piel: 2 Kings 25:7); nechushtayim,
bronze fetters, the same word as Samson's.
Shelf: Henry on 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 39; Josephus X.8 (the two prophecies); Smith's on Zedekiah and on
Blindness (blinding as policy); Levy 1872 on blinding as a punishment in the ancient world; the encyclopedia
"Riblah".
Brian's own notes (20 October): sounds he cannot unhear since losing his sight, which will always stay with him (his dog tearing its ACL; the surgeon cursing while operating on his eye), beside the last thing Zedekiah saw, which the blinding made permanent; and that many blind people would rather stay blind than give up their hearing to get their sight back.
Suggested things Brian might say: the one story in this series with nothing to soften it, and what he does with a
passage like that; losing sight as a weapon versus losing it as he did; whether the order of events, sons first,
is the detail that stays with him too.

### 6. Samson in Gaza (Judges 16:4-31; 13:2-5 for the vow)

Spine: the one man in Scripture blinded in the prime of his strength, in a moment. The text follows what comes
after: the mill, being led by the hand, put on display for people who are laughing, asking the boy to let him feel
the pillars. His last prayer names the loss, "for my two eyes", and God hears it.
Questions to answer: how long between the blinding and the temple? (Long enough for hair to grow: months.) What was
the mill? (Smith's on the hand mill; Henry and Josephus on grinding as the lowest labour.) Who was the boy, and what
did Samson say to him? (Josephus adds words.) What does "the dead he killed at his death were more than he killed in
his life" leave a blind listener with?
Imagined scenes: the prison mill at Gaza, the hair growing back unnoticed by his keepers; the walk into the temple
led by the boy, the noise of the crowd, the hands finding the pillars.
Scene voices: Samson; the boy.
The word: naqar, gouged out (16:21), also used of digging out a well or quarrying stone; tachan, grind; the phrase
"for my two eyes" (shetey enay).
Shelf: Henry on Judges 16 (he is sharp on the laughing crowd and on the prayer); Josephus V.8 (the words to the
boy); Smith's on Gaza and on Mill; Levy 1872, "Samson" (a blind man on the one blinded man); the encyclopedia
"Samson".
Suggested things Brian might say: that God hears a prayer that names the loss plainly; being led by the hand by
someone much younger; repetitive labour done by touch; the difference between Samson's anger and his own peace,
and whether he is allowed both.

## Maps

One map per episode, as before: Beersheba and Hebron for Isaac; Goshen (off-map, south-west) for Jacob, with

for Zedekiah; Gaza and Sorek for Samson. Beersheba, Gaza and Shiloh are already in places.json.
