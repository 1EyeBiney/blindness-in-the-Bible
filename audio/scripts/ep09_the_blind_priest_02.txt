[NARRATOR] (calm) First, where we are. Leviticus chapter twenty-one, at Sinai. This chapter is not for Israel in general. It is for the priests, the descendants of Aaron, the one family who could offer sacrifice. It covers whom a priest may marry, whom he may mourn, and, in our verses, who among them may serve at the altar. Keep the size of this in mind. It concerns a few hundred men of one family and one duty, not blind people as a whole. Leviticus chapter 21, verses 16 to 24.
[READER] (plainly) Then the LORD said to Moses, “Say to Aaron, ‘For the generations to come, none of your descendants who has a physical defect may approach to offer the food of his God. No man who has any defect may approach—no man who is blind, lame, disfigured, or deformed; no man who has a broken foot or hand, or who is a hunchback or dwarf, or who has an eye defect, a festering rash, scabs, or a crushed testicle. No descendant of Aaron the priest who has a defect shall approach to present the food offerings to the LORD. Since he has a defect, he is not to come near to offer the food of his God. He may eat the most holy food of his God as well as the holy food, but because he has a defect, he must not go near the veil or approach the altar, so as not to desecrate My sanctuaries. For I am the LORD who sanctifies them.’” Moses told this to Aaron and his sons and to all the Israelites.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Hear what the passage is careful to say. He may not approach to offer the food of his God. He may not go near the veil or the altar. But he may eat the most holy food as well as the holy. He remains a priest and is fed as one. He does not perform the altar service. And the reason given is the holiness of the sanctuary, not the worth of the man. Many readers have still found it hard, and Brian's thoughts on it belong here more than anyone's. We will come to them.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the word. The Hebrew translated defect is mum. Say it: moom. Strong's gives it as a blemish, physically or morally. The 1915 encyclopedia says it names no particular disease but any disfiguring mark, even scratches and scars, and from there, any moral fault. Listen to where else the Bible uses it. Here is Absalom, David's son. 2 Samuel chapter 14, verse 25.
[READER] (plainly) Now there was not a man in all Israel as handsome and highly praised as Absalom. From the sole of his foot to the top of his head, he did not have a single flaw.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Not a single flaw. That is mum. And here is the bride in the Song of Solomon. Song of Solomon chapter 4, verse 7.
[READER] (plainly) You are altogether beautiful, my darling; in you there is no flaw.
[NARRATOR] (calm) No flaw. Mum again. So the word that keeps the blind priest from the altar is the word for what the most beautiful man and the most beloved woman in the Old Testament did not have. It is a word about appearance before it is a word about anything else. Hold that thought, because Henry will come back to it. And here is the thing to notice above all. It is the same word in the very next chapter, for the animal that may not be put on the altar. Leviticus chapter 22, verse 20.
[READER] (plainly) You must not present anything with a defect, because it will not be accepted on your behalf.
[NARRATOR] (calm) The priest with a mum does not approach. The lamb with a mum is not accepted. The Law asked the same thing of the man who offered and of the thing he offered. And the encyclopedia follows the word all the way to its end, in the New Testament. 1 Peter chapter 1, verses 18 to 19.
[READER] (plainly) For you know that it was not with perishable things such as silver or gold that you were redeemed from the empty way of life you inherited from your forefathers, but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot.
[NARRATOR] (calm) A lamb without blemish or spot. The word that kept the blind priest from the altar is the word that describes the one who was offered on it.

And in verse twenty, the man with an eye defect has a teballul, which Strong's and the encyclopedia both give as a cataract, a white spot in the eye. So the list reached past the man who could not see at all, to the man whose sight was clouded. The spectrum of blindness, which the Bible rarely marks, is marked here, in the one law that limits what a blind person may do.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Now Brian's three questions, in order. First: what did a priest actually do? Most of it with his hands. Smith's dictionary lists the duties: keep the altar fire burning day and night, feed the lamp with oil, offer the morning and evening sacrifices, teach the law, and judge hard cases. Leviticus chapter one has the priests catch and sprinkle the blood, flay and cut up the animal, and lay the pieces on the fire. Touch, all of it. But some duties needed eyes. Leviticus thirteen has the priest look on a skin disease, again and again, and judge whether it is deeper than the skin. And burning the incense meant standing alone in the Holy Place and watching. Edersheim describes Zechariah's turn at it, in the first chapter of Luke, waiting until he saw the incense kindling. Second: would a blind priest touch what he should not? Brian touches things all day to know what they are. Here is the surprise. The rules about touching holy things are for the Levites, not the priests. When the camp moved, Aaron's sons covered the ark and the vessels first, and only then could the Kohathites carry them, and they were told not to touch any holy thing, lest they die. The priests handled the holy things. The carriers were forbidden to. And Leviticus twenty-one keeps the blemished priest from exactly the two places where the fire and the handling were: the veil and the altar. No source on the shelf says a word about accidental touching by a man who cannot see. That worry is Brian's, and it is a good one, but the text does not share it. Third: would one blind priest have burdened the rest? The numbers say no. The priests served in twenty-four courses, each on duty one week in turn, and the lot decided who did what, not anyone's choice. Zechariah, a man of at least sixty, had never before drawn the incense. Edersheim reckons about fifty priests on duty each weekday. One blind man among fifty, in a course that came round twice a year, kept from two tasks and welcome at the rest, would hardly have been noticed.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the harder question, the one under Brian's three. Why the rule at all? Leviticus gives no reason beyond the holiness of the sanctuary. Here is Matthew Henry, in seventeen hundred and six, first on what the law protected.
[BOOKS] (calm) The blemishes were such as they could not help, and therefore, though they might not work, they must not starve. Note, None must be abused for their natural infirmities.
[NARRATOR] (calm) And then Henry supplies a reason of his own, and it is not about the man.
[BOOKS] (calm) But it was especially requisite that comely men should be chosen to minister about holy things, for the sake of the people, who were apt to judge according to outward appearance, and to think meanly of the service.
[NARRATOR] (calm) For the sake of the people, who judge by appearance. Henry's reason, not Leviticus's. Remember Absalom and the bride: mum is a word about how a thing looks. Josephus, in the first century, says the same two things in one sentence.
[BOOKS] (calm) He ordered that the priest who had any blemish, should have his portion indeed among the priests, but he forbade him to ascend the altar, or to enter into the holy house.
[NARRATOR] (calm) The rule was still read literally a thousand years after Sinai. Josephus records that in the first century before Christ, a king named Antigonus took his rival, the high priest Hyrcanus, and cut off his ears, for one reason.
[BOOKS] (calm) He cut off his ears, and thereby took care that the high priesthood should never come to him any more, because he was maimed, while the law required that this dignity should belong to none but such as had all their members entire.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Later rules went further than Leviticus. The 1915 encyclopedia says a priest blind even in one eye was not to pronounce the priestly blessing. Edersheim, describing the synagogue, says the blessing could not be given by those so blind as not to be able to discern daylight, and he gives the reason the rabbis gave.
[BOOKS] (calm) According to the Mishnah, they who pronounce the benediction must have no blemish on their hands, face, or feet, so as not to attract attention.
[NARRATOR] (calm) So as not to attract attention. The Talmud adds that if the townspeople were used to the man, he was allowed. The worry was that people would stare. It is a small window onto the real question, which was never the man's ability. It was what the people in front of him were used to. Brian is about to say that this has not changed.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Brian, with the last word. His three questions answered, and then what he can speak to.
[BRIAN] (calm) Reading this law, I had three questions, and the section on the place and the time now answers them. I wanted to know what a priest actually did, and it turns out that most of it was done with the hands: the fire, the lamp, the sacrifices, the blood. I wondered whether a blind priest would touch things he should not, since I touch things all day to know what they are, and I learned that the rules about touching holy things were for the Levites who carried them, not for the priests who handled them. And I asked whether fitting a blind priest in would have burdened the rest, and the numbers say no. Twenty-four courses, a week each, fifty men on duty at a time. One blind man among them would hardly have been noticed. I find it hard to weigh this law as if it were mine to keep. We live on the far side of the cross. Our worship is not built around one altar and one family of priests, and the sacrifice this law guarded has been made once and for all. The word section above says it better than I can: the word that kept the blind priest from the altar is the word for the lamb that was offered on it. So I read this law as what it was, a rule for a few hundred men and one duty, and I do not read it as a word about what blind people are worth. The text itself takes care of that. He stayed a priest, and he ate at the table. What I can speak to is now. I know quite a few blind pastors and spiritual leaders, and I am grateful beyond words that they are allowed to minister and to do God's work with the talents and abilities they have. Nobody keeps them from the pulpit for a defect. I am glad we do not have that requirement. But we do have something else, and in some ways it is more shackling, because nobody wrote it down. It is low expectations. A rule can be read and argued with. An expectation just sits there, in the way people hesitate before handing you a task, or thank you too much for doing an ordinary thing. The old rabbis, arguing about whether a one-eyed priest could give the blessing, worried that the people would stare. One of them allowed it if the town was used to him. That is still the whole question. Not what the blind man can do, but what the people around him expect. So the blind priest of Leviticus and the blind pastor I know are in the same place after all. Both are kept, or freed, less by a law than by what the people in front of them are used to.
