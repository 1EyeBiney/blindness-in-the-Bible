[BRIAN] (calm) Samson lost his sight in the prime of his life. So do a lot of blind adults, and so did I. Most of the people in this series went blind slowly, with age. Samson went blind in a moment, at the height of his strength, and that puts him closer to me and to the people I work with than any of the others. What I think about first is the adjustment. He was the most physically gifted man in the Bible, and in one afternoon he was reduced to being led around by a hand, with his strength gone and his eyes gone with it. The transition was sudden. I had nine months between the diagnosis and the end of my sight, and that was hard. I work all the time with people who lose their vision in a matter of days or hours, and they are the hardest for me to work with, because they had no time to prepare. No time to get their last looks at the things they wanted to remember. Samson got none. The last thing he saw was Delilah's room, and the Philistines in it. The Hebrew word matters to me here in a way it may not to a sighted reader. Judges says the Philistines gouged out his eyes, and the verb is naqar, which the old dictionary defines as to bore, to penetrate, to quarry. Isaiah uses the same word for the quarry a stone is dug from. I have had both of my eyes enucleated, removed, because of the extreme pressure in them before the last surgeries. My doctor described what he was going to do as scooping out the inside of a melon. That is naqar. I want to say plainly that it was one of my better decisions in life. My eyes had stopped working completely and the pain and discomfort went with them. Samson had no say in it and no anaesthetic, and I know more than I would like about what was done to him. Then there is purpose. Samson had been the judge and protector of Israel for twenty years, and blindness did not end that. The text says his hair began to grow back, and then it shows him in the temple at Gaza, asking the servant who held his hand to put him where he could feel the pillars. With nobody left to help him, he asked God for help, and God gave him just enough strength, once more, to finish what he had been given to do. Blind, he still found his purpose and did it. The book says he did more in his death than in his life.

[BOOKS] (calm) Eyeless in Gaza at the mill with slaves.
[NARRATOR] (calm) A blind poet's line about a blind judge, kept by a blind writer. Matthew Henry, who could see, read the prison and the hall with less pity and more care than most. [NARRATOR] (calm) Henry is right about the difference, and the text does not hide it; Samson says for my two eyes. But the hair is where Henry lands, and it is where the text lands too. The growth of it, he says, was neither the cause nor the sign of the return of his strength, except as the badge of his consecration, a token that God accepted him as a Nazirite again. The strength that he had lost by sin, Henry says, he, like a true penitent, recovers by prayer. Josephus adds only that Samson told the boy he was weary and wanted to rest, which is the text's own sentence with the meaning filled in.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the last word in the chain, and it is the hardest one. Zedekiah's was "ah-VAR," blinded. Isaac's was "kah-HAH," dim. Jacob's was "kah-VED," heavy. Eli's and Ahijah's was "KOOM," stood still. Samson's is "nah-KAR." To bore. To dig out. To quarry. It is used six times in the whole Bible, and it is never gentle. It is the word for the quarry a stone is cut from, in Isaiah. It is the word for what the ravens do to an eye, in Proverbs. It is the word Nahash the Ammonite used when he offered the men of Jabesh a treaty on condition of their right eyes. It is a stonemason's word, and Judges uses it for a man's face. Brian has told you why it matters to him. It is the one word in the chain he knows from the inside. And beside it, in the same verse, is the word that closes the loop. Bronze. Nechosheth. Samson was bound in it, and some five hundred years later so was the last king of Judah, and the Bible describes exactly those two men that way: blinded, then bound in bronze, then led. We began with the one and we end with the other. A king who had everything and would not listen, and a judge who had everything and would not either. Both of them in the dark, in chains, in an enemy's city. And then the two stories part. Zedekiah was given a promise he had not earned and died in peace. Samson was given his strength once more and died with his hands on the pillars. Six men, six words, and the chain is complete: put out, grown dim, grown heavy, stood still, stood still, dug out. Not one of them saw again. Not one of them is finished in the text when his eyes are.
