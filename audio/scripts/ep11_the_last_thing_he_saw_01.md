# Not By Sight, episode 11: The last thing he saw

**Narrator** *(calm)* Before the story, a word about sound.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Light is easy to stop. Close a door and the room behind it is gone. Turn a corner and the street behind you is gone. Shut your eyes and everything is gone. Sound is not like that. Sound bends round the corner and comes under the door.

*(SFX, if you can: a voice talking in another room, behind a closed door, muffled, three seconds, under the next line)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* You cannot make out the words. But you know there is a person, and whether they are calm, and roughly how far away. The door stopped the light. The sound? It only got softened.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Sound almost always means that something just happened. A release, or a collision. Energy went somewhere.

*(SFX, words carry it: one firecracker, outdoors, with its echo, two seconds)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* A firecracker is a release. A footstep is a collision, the smallest kind, repeated.

*(SFX, if you can: footsteps crossing a wooden floor, then onto tile, four seconds, under the next line)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Nobody can walk silently. Every step is a small impact, and every impact makes a sound, and the sound tells you where the person is, how heavy, how fast, and whether they are coming toward you. Footsteps run through this whole series. You will hear them at a door in Shiloh, and on the way to two pillars in Gaza.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* And sound comes back. Clap your hands in a stairwell and the stairwell answers.

*(SFX, if you can: a single clap in a large hard space, with a long return, three seconds)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Some blind people learn to use that on purpose, and all of them use it without meaning to. A room sounds its size. A wall sounds like a wall before you reach it.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* God made light and called it good. He made sound too, and a blind man will tell you he is grateful for every one of these properties, every day. Brian puts it this way.

**Brian** *(calm)* Ask a room full of blind people whether they would give up their hearing to get their sight back. Most of them would not. I have thought about it more than once, and I'm not a talker, so I don't really know which I would choose myself.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Sound also has one more property, and it is the one this episode is about. You can close your eyes. You cannot close your ears. Sound comes in whether you want it or not, and some sounds you would give almost anything to not hear.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Those are the easy ones. Brian has a few of his own, and they are not funny. Here is Mister Scaredy Cat again. The voice is borrowed. The words are Brian's.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Brian** *(calm)* Whatever we open the episode with, this is going to be a hard one to talk about.

**Brian** *(calm)* There are sounds you cannot unhear. I heard my dog cry out in pain when he jumped off the deck and tore his ACL. He went blind from the knee surgery that followed, which I thought was a little unfair. I heard my doctor curse while he was operating on my eye. That was the last day I had any usable vision. You cannot look away from a sound and pretend it is not there. A sighted person can turn their head. The sound comes in anyway, and it stays.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* A sound that stays. Hold on to that, because this is the story of a man whose last sight was chosen for him, and arranged so that it would stay.

**Narrator** *(calm)* This is Not By Sight, a series on blindness in the Bible, made by a blind Christian for blind believers, their families, and the church. The Scripture you will hear is the Berean Standard Bible, read word for word. The thoughts marked as Brian's are Brian Clark's own. And when we imagine a scene, we will tell you so.

**Narrator** *(calm)* This episode is part of our series on sight lost and not given back: six people in the Bible who lost their sight and lived the rest of their lives without it. We tell them in three pairs. Two men blinded by their enemies, Zedekiah and Samson. A father and a son, Isaac and Jacob. Two old men of Shiloh, Eli and Ahijah. Every episode begins with sounds, and with what a blind man hears in them. Along the way we follow the Hebrew words the Bible uses for eyes that fail, one word handing on to the next. When we imagine a scene, we will tell you so. Everything else is from the text, from the old books read in their own voice, and from Brian.

**Narrator** *(calm)* First, where we are. Jerusalem, about six hundred years before Christ, and the last king who will ever reign in it. His name is Zedekiah. He was put on the throne by the king of Babylon, at twenty-one, after Babylon had carried the previous king away. He reigned eleven years. He rebelled, against the repeated warnings of the prophet Jeremiah, who was in the city the whole time, and for part of it in a cistern. Babylon came back. The siege lasted a year and a half. By the end the people were starving.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Three places matter. Jerusalem, where it begins. Riblah, far to the north in the land of Hamath, on the great road between Palestine and Babylon, where the king of Babylon stayed while his armies worked in the west. And Babylon itself, weeks further on. Keep those three in order.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* The story is told three times in Scripture, in nearly the same words each time. We will read it from Second Kings, chapter 25, verses 1 to 7.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* So in the ninth year of Zedekiah’s reign, on the tenth day of the tenth month, Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon marched against Jerusalem with his entire army. They encamped outside the city and built a siege wall all around it.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And the city was kept under siege until King Zedekiah’s eleventh year.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* By the ninth day of the fourth month, the famine in the city was so severe that the people of the land had no food.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then the city was breached; and though the Chaldeans had surrounded the city, all the men of war fled by night by way of the gate between the two walls near the king’s garden. They headed toward the Arabah,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* but the army of the Chaldeans pursued the king and overtook him in the plains of Jericho, and his whole army deserted him.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* The Chaldeans seized the king and brought him up to the king of Babylon at Riblah, where they pronounced judgment on him.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And they slaughtered the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes. Then they put out his eyes, bound him with bronze shackles, and took him to Babylon.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* One verse. The text says it and moves on. We are not going to move on quite so quickly, but we are going to be careful. Nothing in this episode will be described that the text does not describe.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Before the end, it is worth knowing what kind of man this was, because the book of Jeremiah tells us, and it is not what you might expect. Zedekiah was not a wicked king in the mould of the ones before him. He was a frightened one. Three times during the siege he sent for Jeremiah in secret. Jeremiah chapter 37, verse 17.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Later, King Zedekiah sent for Jeremiah and received him in his palace, where he asked him privately, “Is there a word from the LORD?” “There is,” Jeremiah replied. “You will be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon.”

**Narrator** *(calm)* He kept asking. The last time, he met the prophet at a side entrance of the Temple and swore an oath to protect him. Jeremiah chapter 38, verses 14 to 17.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then King Zedekiah sent for Jeremiah the prophet and received him at the third entrance to the house of the LORD. “I am going to ask you something,” said the king to Jeremiah. “Do not hide anything from me.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “If I tell you,” Jeremiah replied, “you will surely put me to death. And even if I give you advice, you will not listen to me.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But King Zedekiah swore secretly to Jeremiah, “As surely as the LORD lives, who has given us this life, I will not kill you, nor will I deliver you into the hands of these men who are seeking your life.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then Jeremiah said to Zedekiah, “This is what the LORD God of Hosts, the God of Israel, says: ‘If you indeed surrender to the officers of the king of Babylon, then you will live, this city will not be burned down, and you and your household will survive.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Surrender, and you will live, and your household with you. He was told plainly how to keep his sons. And the conversation ends with the king asking the prophet to keep it secret, on pain of death, because he was afraid of his own officials. Jeremiah chapter 38, verse 24.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then Zedekiah warned Jeremiah, “Do not let anyone know about this conversation, or you will die.

**Narrator** *(calm)* A man who kept asking for the word of the LORD and never once acted on it. That is who went out through the gate.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Imagine the night the wall was breached.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

*(SFX, if you can: night, a city under siege, distant and low; then a wooden gate opened carefully; many feet, hurrying, trying to be quiet; no voices)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* No torches. A gate on the far side of the city from the breach, between two walls, by the king's garden. The king and what is left of his army go through it in the dark and head down toward the Jordan valley, the Arabah. Imagine what that is like for a sighted man. He cannot see the road either. He is listening, the whole way down, for horses behind him.

*(SFX, if you can: the feet fade; a long pause; then, far off, horses)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* They caught him in the plains of Jericho, at dawn, the old dictionary says. His army scattered. And they took him north, not to Babylon yet, but to Riblah, to the king of Babylon, for sentence.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* We are not going to imagine Riblah. The text puts three things in order there, and the order is the whole cruelty. First, his sons were killed in front of him. Then his eyes were put out. Then he was bound in bronze and taken away. Sons first, so that he would see it. Eyes second, so that it would be the last thing he ever saw. Matthew Henry, three hundred years ago, saw exactly what was being done: the point of the order was that the last sight his eyes ever took in would stay on him, as grief and horror, for as long as he lived. And of the blinding itself Henry said that Zedekiah ended his days before he ended his life.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Then the road. From Riblah to Babylon is a journey of weeks. He walked it, or was carried, in chains, blind, through country he had never seen, into the greatest city in the world, which he would never see. Henry noticed the chains, and his point was a blind man's point: Zedekiah did not need binding, his blindness bound him. They chained him anyway, for the disgrace of it. The last of the three accounts, in Jeremiah chapter 52, adds the one thing Kings leaves out: he was kept in custody in Babylon until his dying day.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. Zedekiah brings back a lot of memories of pain and discomfort. I do not like to talk about it, but if there is an episode for it, this is the one.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. People do not realise how violent the treatment of a failing eye is. I had [nine months / how long] between the diagnosis and the end of my sight, and most of that time was spent trying to save it. Scraping the surface of the eye. Replacing parts of it. Needles into it, more than once. Injections I could feel going in and medications I could feel working. Such extreme measures for such a delicate instrument. The pressure inside my eyes climbed until [what it felt like], and there is nothing to do with that kind of pain except get through the day and hope the next one is better. There are a great many more nerves in the eye than most people know, and upsetting them was worse than anything else I have been through, and that includes heart bypass surgery.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. The last operation was the one where I heard the surgeon curse. I knew before anyone told me. That was the last day I had any usable vision. After that the eyes were not just useless, they were a source of pain with no purpose left, and in the end I had both of them removed. People wince when I say that. It was one of the better decisions of my life. The pain went with them. I do not have to worry about eye pain any more, and I am so grateful for that. Many people who lose their sight still live with discomfort and pain long after the sight is gone. So I know it could be worse.

**Brian** *(calm)* I do not even want to think about what Zedekiah faced. The last thing he saw was out of his worst nightmares, and then, with all of that pain, he was made to walk from Riblah to Babylon, a journey of weeks, to the prison where he would die. Nobody was trying to save his eyes. Nobody gave him anything for it. The text says it in a single verse and moves on. I cannot move on from it that quickly.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the prophets, and a question the old books could not leave alone. Two prophets had spoken about this king, and they seemed to contradict each other. Jeremiah, in the city, during the siege, told Zedekiah to his face what would happen. Jeremiah chapter 34, verse 3.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And you yourself will not escape his grasp, but will surely be captured and delivered into his hand. You will see the king of Babylon eye to eye and speak with him face to face; and you will go to Babylon.

**Narrator** *(calm)* You will see the king of Babylon, eye to eye. Now Ezekiel. He was already in Babylon, among the first exiles, and years before the siege God had him act this out in front of them. Ezekiel chapter 12, verses 5 and 6.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* As they watch, dig through the wall and carry your belongings out through it.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And as they watch, lift your bags to your shoulder and take them out at dusk; cover your face so that you cannot see the land. For I have made you a sign to the house of Israel.”

**Narrator** *(calm)* A prophet dug through a wall at dusk, carrying his baggage, with his face covered so that he could not see, while the exiles watched. Then God told them what it meant. Verses 12 and 13.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And at dusk the prince among them will lift his bags to his shoulder and go out. They will dig through the wall to bring him out. He will cover his face so he cannot see the land.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But I will spread My net over him, and he will be caught in My snare. I will bring him to Babylon, the land of the Chaldeans; yet he will not see it, and there he will die.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Years before it happened, in a foreign country, a man performed Zedekiah's blindness in the street. He will go out at dusk. He will not see the land. He will be brought to Babylon, and he will not see it. Set that beside Jeremiah: he will see the king of Babylon, eye to eye. Both cannot be true. Unless the blinding happens in between. Henry put it in one sentence.

**Henry** *(calm)* By this two prophecies that seemed to contradict one another were both fulfilled. Jeremiah prophesied that Zedekiah should be brought to Babylon; Ezekiel prophesied that he should not see Babylon. He was brought thither, but, his eyes being put out, he did not see it.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* At Riblah, he stood in front of the king of Babylon with his eyes. On the road after Riblah, he had none. The order of events that was the cruelty was also, to the letter, what two prophets had said.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the word. Each of these six stories has its own word for eyes that fail, and across the series they make a chain. This story's word is the first link, and it is the hardest one: "ah-VAR." To blind. The verb under "iv-VAIR," the plain Hebrew word for a blind man. Three times the Bible tells what was done to Zedekiah, and three times it uses this verb. He is the only person in the Old Testament of whom it is used literally, of a thing done to a man's eyes.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Its other two uses are in the Law, and they are not about eyes at all. They are about bribes. Exodus says a bribe blinds those who see. Deuteronomy says a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise. The word that describes what Babylon did to the king is the word Moses used for what a gift in the wrong hand does to a judge. If you heard our Law series, you have met that idea before.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* And a second word, because it points forward. The shackles. All three accounts name them, and the word, in its paired form, means two pieces of bronze. There is exactly one other man in the Bible bound in that word after being blinded. He is Samson, four hundred years earlier, in Gaza. A king and a judge, the two men the Bible says were blinded by their enemies, both led away in bronze. He is the last story in this series. We begin and end with the two men in chains.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* The Bible does not say how it was done, for Zedekiah or for Samson. The old books guessed, and we will not repeat the guesses. What the text does give is what came after, and it is not nothing. Jeremiah had made Zedekiah a second promise in the same breath as the first. Jeremiah chapter 34, verses 4 and 5.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Yet hear the word of the LORD, O Zedekiah king of Judah. This is what the LORD says concerning you: You will not die by the sword;

> **Reader** *(plainly)* you will die in peace. As spices were burned for your fathers, the former kings who preceded you, so people will burn spices for you and lament, ‘Alas, O master!’ For I Myself have spoken this word, declares the LORD.”

**Narrator** *(calm)* He did not die by the sword. The record says he was kept in custody until the day of his death, and nothing more. Whether they burned spices for him in Babylon, the text does not say.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* And the same chapter that tells of the king's blinding tells what happened to the prophet, on the same campaign, by order of the same conqueror. Jeremiah chapter 39, verses 11 and 12.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Now Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon had given orders about Jeremiah through Nebuzaradan captain of the guard, saying,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “Take him, look after him, and do not let any harm come to him; do for him whatever he says.”

**Narrator** *(calm)* And verse 14 ends: So Jeremiah remained among his own people. The prophet who warned went home. The king who kept asking and never listened went to Babylon blind. The text does not comment. It only puts the two of them in the same chapter.
