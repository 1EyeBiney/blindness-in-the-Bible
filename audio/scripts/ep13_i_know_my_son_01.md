**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. Isaac asked who was there, and he was lied to. Jacob asked who was there, and he was told the truth. Same question, same family, one generation apart. Asking is not the hard part. Every blind person asks who is in the room. The hard part is what comes after. Somebody answers you, and you have to decide whether to believe them. Isaac believed a lie and gave away the blessing. Jacob believed the truth, and then went on to trust his own hands over his son's eyes. Two blind men, two answers, two very different endings. That is what this episode is about.

**Narrator** *(calm)* First, where we are, and it takes a whole life to say, because Jacob spent his whole life learning one thing: that eyes cannot be trusted. He learned it first by using it. In his father's tent he put on goatskin and his brother's clothes and lied to a blind man, and the blind man's hands and nose and mouth all told him the lie was true. Jacob walked out with the blessing and learned that a man who cannot see can be fooled by everything else.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Then it was done to him. He ran to Haran and worked seven years for Rachel, and on the wedding night his uncle put the wrong sister into his bed in the dark. When morning came, there was Leah. The dark had done to Jacob what blindness had done to his father, and when he asked why he had been deceived, Laban's answer was about the younger and the older. Jacob was being paid back in his own coin.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Twenty years later, going home, he spent a night alone by the river Jabbok wrestling a man in the night, until daybreak. Genesis never says what the man looked like, and when Jacob asked his name he did not get it. Yet when it was over he named the place Peniel, the face of God, and said, I have seen God face to face. A night, a stranger with no name, a question not answered, and Jacob called it seeing. Seeing was already something other than eyes to him.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Then his eyes lied to him outright. His sons brought him Joseph's robe dipped in the blood of a goat, and said, examine it. He looked at it, and he recognized it, and he said, Joseph has surely been torn to pieces. His eyes were right about the robe and wrong about everything that mattered, and he believed them for twenty years. The man who had fooled his father with a garment and a goat was fooled by his sons with a garment and a goat.

**Narrator** *(calm)* And then the wagons. He was a hundred and thirty. His sons came back from Egypt and told him Joseph was alive and ruling the whole land, and the text says he was stunned, because he did not believe them. True words, and he would not take them. What changed his mind was not what they said. It was what he saw. Imagine that afternoon in Hebron.

**Narrator** *(calm)* An old man standing outside his tent, his sons around him still talking, and he is not listening to them. He is looking down the road. Something is coming up it that he has never seen in his life: wagons, Egyptian wagons, big ones, the kind nobody in Canaan owns, and the dust of a long train behind them.

**Israel** *(old, half to himself)* Words. You can bring me words all day. Those are wagons.

**Narrator** *(calm)* That is imagined. What Genesis says is that when he saw the wagons Joseph had sent to carry him back, the spirit of their father Jacob was revived, and he said, enough, my son Joseph is still alive, I will go to see him before I die. So at a hundred and thirty, after the tent, and the dark, and the river, and the robe, he was still a man who had to see a thing before he would believe it. He went down to Egypt, and when he met Joseph he said, finally I can die, now that I have seen your face.

**Narrator** *(calm)* He did not die. He lived seventeen more years in Goshen, and in those years his eyes went. By the chapter we are reading he is a hundred and forty-seven, he has made Joseph swear to carry him home to the family grave, and he is ill. Joseph is told, and he comes, and he brings his two sons. Keep the whole chain in mind, because this chapter is the end of it. For once, Jacob cannot see. And for once, he does not need to.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Where this is. Stand in Goshen, in the green north-east of Egypt, where the Nile spreads out toward the sea, and face north, with north at twelve o'clock. Nothing from the rest of this series is near you. The land of the promise is a long way off at two o'clock, and three places in it are named in this one chapter. Hebron, where Abraham and Isaac lie in the cave Jacob has just made Joseph swear to carry him to, is about two weeks' walk at two o'clock. Bethlehem, where he buried Rachel beside the road, is a few hours beyond it on the same line. And Bethel, which he still calls Luz, where God appeared to him on his way out with nothing, is a day past that. Keep those three in mind. A blind man in Egypt is about to name every one of them from his bed.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Hear what he did in those verses, because it is a legal act. Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, like Reuben and Simeon. He adopted his two grandsons as his own sons, which is why there are tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh in Israel and no tribe of Joseph, and why Joseph, through them, got two shares of the land where his brothers got one. Matthew Henry calls it a charter of adoption. And hear who those two boys were. Joseph had been made an Egyptian to serve Pharaoh: an Egyptian name, Zaphenath-paneah, and an Egyptian wife, Asenath, the daughter of the priest of On, the city of the sun god. Ephraim and Manasseh were half Egyptian, grandsons of a pagan priest, born in a country whose people would not sit at a table with a Hebrew. Jacob names the one thing that could have kept them out, born to you in Egypt, and overrides it with a word. Their mother's blood did not decide what they were. Their grandfather did. Why two shares for one son? Because it is the firstborn's double portion. Reuben was the firstborn and lost it, Chronicles says, for defiling his father's bed, and the birthright went to the sons of Joseph, the firstborn of Rachel, the wife Jacob had served for. The way you give one son a double share of land is to make his two sons two tribes. Then Luz, and the road to Bethlehem. He has not seen either in seventeen years and will not see them again, and he places them from his bed as exactly as a man who still walks them. And then, verse 8, having just named the two boys and made them his, he asks.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Israel saw the sons of Joseph, he asked, “Who are these?”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Joseph said to his father, “They are the sons God has given me in this place.” So Jacob said, “Please bring them to me, that I may bless them.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Now Israel’s eyesight was poor because of old age; he could hardly see. Joseph brought his sons to him, and his father kissed them and embraced them.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “I never expected to see your face again,” Israel said to Joseph, “but now God has let me see your children as well.”

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Two verses, and they seem to disagree. Verse 8 says Israel saw the sons of Joseph. Verse 10 says he could hardly see. Put them together and you have a man who can tell there are two shapes in the room and cannot tell whose they are. He had named them both, Ephraim and Manasseh, a moment before. Now he cannot tell which shape is which. Many blind people live exactly there, and Brian will have something to say about it. Some readers think the question was also part of the ceremony, a father formally asking who stands before him before he blesses them, the way Isaac asked who are you, and Joseph's answer does sound formal: the sons God has given me in this place. The text does not say that. What it does say, in the very next verse, is why he asked. So he does what Isaac did. He asks who they are, and he has them brought close, and he kisses them and holds them. The hands again. And then he says a thing that only a man in his condition would put that way: God has let me see your children. He cannot make out their faces. He is holding them. That is what seeing means to him now. Verses 12 to 20.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then Joseph removed his sons from his father’s knees and bowed facedown.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And Joseph took both of them—with Ephraim in his right hand toward Israel’s left hand, and Manasseh in his left hand toward Israel’s right hand—and brought them close to him.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But Israel stretched out his right hand and put it on the head of Ephraim, the younger; and crossing his hands, he put his left on Manasseh’s head, although Manasseh was the firstborn.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then he blessed Joseph and said: “May the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* the angel who has redeemed me from all harm—may He bless these boys. And may they be called by my name and the names of my fathers Abraham and Isaac, and may they grow into a multitude upon the earth.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Joseph saw that his father had placed his right hand on Ephraim’s head, he was displeased and took his father’s hand to move it from Ephraim’s head to Manasseh’s.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “Not so, my father!” Joseph said. “This one is the firstborn; put your right hand on his head.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But his father refused. “I know, my son, I know!” he said. “He too shall become a people, and he too shall be great; nevertheless, his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his offspring shall become a multitude of nations.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* So that day Jacob blessed them and said: “By you shall Israel pronounce this blessing: ‘May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh.’” So he put Ephraim before Manasseh.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Count what Joseph did, because Joseph is the sighted man in the room and he did everything a sighted man does. He placed the boys by eye, the older to his father's right hand, the younger to his left, so that a blind man reaching straight out could not get it wrong. He watched the hands. When he saw them cross, he reached in and took hold of his father's hand to put it where it belonged. Every one of those is kindness. Every one of those is also the assumption that the man in the bed did not know what he was doing. And now count what Jacob did. He knew which boy was under which hand, and nobody had told him. He knew the custom: a father sets the firstborn to the right. He knew where Joseph had set them. And he had already held them both, and one was bigger. He crossed his arms on purpose, and the Hebrew word for what he did with his hands means to act with understanding, the way the old translation has it, guiding his hands wittingly. And when the hand came in to correct him, he did not let it. Notice how verse 17 begins: when Joseph saw. In the whole chapter that is the last time anyone trusts his eyes, and it is the sighted man, and he is the one who is wrong.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now imagine that one moment, the one the text gives in half a verse. Joseph took his father's hand to move it.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

*(SFX, if you can: the sickroom again. Very quiet. Two boys trying not to move.)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Jacob's hands are on two heads. He knows which is which, because Joseph set them the way every father does, firstborn to the right, and because he has held them, and the one under his right hand is smaller. The blessing is half said. And then he feels a grip on his right wrist. Not a boy's hand. A man's, strong, careful, a hand that has signed for the grain of Egypt. He knows that hand. He has not felt it on him since it was a boy's hand, in Hebron, before the robe. It is lifting his hand off the smaller head, gently, the way you move an old man's hand off a thing he has got wrong.

**Joseph** *(low, firm)* Not so, my father. This one is the firstborn. Put your right hand on his head.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Israel** *(old, unhurried)* I know, my son. I know.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* I know. Seventy years earlier, in another room, a sighted son's hand went out to a blind father's and the blind father was fooled by it. This time the hand goes out to correct, not to deceive, and the blind father is not moved by it. He had not made a mistake. He had made a choice, and he had made it with the only thing anyone has ever been able to make that choice with, which is not eyes. Isaac blessed the wrong son by accident and let it stand. Jacob blessed the younger son on purpose and would not be talked out of it. The same family, the same hands, the same crossing of the older and the younger, and the second blind man is the one who gets it right. That is where the two stories part. Isaac trusted his hands and was deceived. Jacob trusted his hands and was right. The hands were the same. What had changed was that nobody was lying to them, and that the man behind them had spent a lifetime learning what eyes are worth. At the wagons he still had to see to believe. Here he cannot see, and he believes anyway, and acts on it, and will not be moved.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, what do you hear in this one?

*(SFX, if you can: the sickroom from the opening, very quietly under his first line)*

**Brian** *(calm)* Joseph reached in to move his father's hand because he assumed that a man who could not see did not know what he was doing. Jacob's answer was two words, said twice: I know. The Hebrew verb there is yada, and the old dictionary says it means to know, properly to ascertain by seeing. Genesis gives the seeing-verb for knowing to a man who could hardly see, and it gives it to him about what his hands had told him. That is the whole page for me.

**Brian** *(calm)* Blind people are underestimated because sighted people tend to think sight is a vastly superior sense to all the others. I say touch is the most important sense. Think about how much of walking is the sense of touch through your feet, and how much of the rest of the day is your hands and your fingers. We move through this world by a combination of senses. When a fire truck comes along, you hear it long before you see it, and the hearing is what tells you to pull over. Our senses work together to get information to a brain that is locked away from the world behind a thick skull. Jacob's eyes had gone, and his hands, his memory and his judgment had not. He arranged the boys, crossed his arms on purpose, and when the sighted man in the room tried to correct him, he said what every blind person has had to say at some point: I know.

**Brian** *(calm)* Loss of sight does not take understanding with it. It does not take faith, or judgment, or memory, or kindness, or any of the many things that make a person who they are. The writer of Hebrews picked this scene, out of all of Jacob's long life, as his one act of faith: by faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of Joseph's sons. Dying, nearly blind, and that is the moment chosen.

**Brian** *(calm)* One more thing I noticed. Genesis does not use the same word for Isaac's sight loss and for Jacob's. Isaac's eyes were kahah, weak, grown dim. Jacob's were kabed, heavy, which is the Bible's word for a burden. I know both of those. Some days sight loss is a dimming you learn to live around. Some days it is a weight you carry. Jacob carried his to the end of the chapter and blessed twelve sons by name with it on him.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. And one more. Jacob did not see clearly for most of his life, and he saw clearly at the end. His eyes fooled him about the robe, the dark fooled him about his wife, and at the wagons he still would not believe good news until he had seen it. Then, when his eyes could hardly see at all, he put his hands on two boys and knew which was which, and would not be corrected. The one time his sight was gone was the one time he got it right. You cannot always trust your eyes. Jacob learned that the hard way, and so did I.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the old books. Matthew Henry noticed the same two things Brian did, that Jacob went blind the way his father did, and that the word is a heavy one. He put them in one breath.

**Henry** *(calm)* Those that have the honour of age must therewith be content to take the burden of it. The eye of faith may be very clear even when the eye of the body is very much clouded.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian put that second sentence on his page, because it is what the story is about. Henry goes on to the crossed hands, and he is careful about what they were not. Jacob did not cross them by mistake, he says, nor in a humour, nor from a partial affection to one boy more than the other, but from a spirit of prophecy, in compliance with the divine counsels. Then he makes a list, and it is worth hearing, because Jacob is on it twice. God, he says, has so often advanced the younger above the elder: Abel above Cain, Isaac above Ishmael, Jacob above Esau, Ephraim above Manasseh, David above his elder brothers. He tied the Jews to observe the birthright, Henry says, but he never tied himself to observe it. Which means the crossed hands were not a blind man's confusion. They were the family pattern, and the one man in the room who could not see was the one who could see it.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the word. Each of these six stories has its own word for eyes that fail, and they make a chain. Zedekiah's word was "ah-VAR," to blind, a thing done to a man. Isaac's was "kah-HAH," to grow dim. Jacob's is a different word again: "kah-VED." Heavy. Verse 10 says, in the Hebrew, that Israel's eyes were heavy from age. It is one of the commonest words in the Bible, and it is almost never about eyes. It is the word for Pharaoh's heart when he hardened it, heavy. It is the word for the people's ears in Isaiah when they would not hear, heavy. And with a slightly different vowel it is kavod, glory, the weight of God's presence. The same root carries the hardest hearts and the glory of God, and once, here, an old man's eyes.

**Narrator** *(calm)* So the chain so far runs: a king's eyes put out, a father's eyes grown dim, a son's eyes grown heavy. Two of those happened the ordinary way, by age, to two men in one family, and Genesis gave each of them his own word. Brian hears the difference between them, a dimming and a weight, and the Bible seems to as well. Next time the chain goes back to Isaac's word, dim, and adds one more, for an old priest whose eyes had stopped moving.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* These are the tribes of Israel, twelve in all, and this was what their father said to them. He blessed them, and he blessed each one with a suitable blessing.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Each one with a suitable blessing. Twelve men, in order, by name, with a word fitted to each, from a man who could not see which of them was standing where. He would have known them by their voices and their places, the way Isaac should have known Jacob. Then he gave the instructions about the cave one more time, and verse 33.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Jacob had finished instructing his sons, he pulled his feet into the bed and breathed his last, and he was gathered to his people.

**Narrator** *(calm)* His sons did as he had charged them. They carried him up out of Egypt, two weeks' walk at two o'clock, and buried him in the cave at Machpelah with Abraham and Isaac. The blind father and the blind son, in one cave. Joseph could not follow them. He was bound to Egypt by his office, and he died there at a hundred and ten, having made the sons of Israel swear to carry his bones up when God brought them out. Four hundred years later they did, and Joshua buried him at Shechem, on the ridge of land Jacob had handed him at the end of this chapter, one portion above his brothers. The adoption of his sons and the oath about his bones were two halves of one thing: a man who could not leave, securing his place in the promise through the people who could. And the New Testament, in the chapter that lists the people who lived by faith, gives the father and the son two verses, back to back. Hebrews chapter 11, verses 20 and 21.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning the future.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* By faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of Joseph’s sons and worshiped as he leaned on the top of his staff.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Two blind men, two blessings, and the Bible's one word for both of them is faith. Isaac's came by faith after his hands had failed him. Jacob's came by faith with his hands doing exactly what he meant them to. And leaning on the top of his staff, which is the one detail Hebrews adds. He was holding on to something while he did it. Blind men usually are.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, with the last word.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. Every blind person I know has had a hand reach in to move theirs. Usually it is kind. Usually the person is sure they are helping. And the hardest two words to say in that moment, without heat, are the ones Jacob said. I know. He did not argue, and he did not explain himself to Joseph until after. He just did not let the hand move his, and he finished the blessing. He had learned in a tent in Beersheba what hands can be made to say, and he spent the rest of his life finding out that eyes are no better. At the end, with both gone, he still knew who was under each hand, and he still knew what God had said. That is what he was leaning on.
