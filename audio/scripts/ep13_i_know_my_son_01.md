**Narrator** *(calm)* Who are these. That is Jacob, the son who stood in the goatskin, and the question is almost his father's. Which one are you. Who are these. Two blind men, two generations apart, asking the same thing of the room. Here is Brian, by way of Mister Scaredy Cat, on that question.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. Who is in the room is the first thing a blind man needs to know and the last thing anyone thinks to tell him. People walk in and assume you know. Children especially. I have asked Jacob's question more times than I can count, and I have learned to ask it the way he did, out loud and early, before anyone has said anything I would have to pretend I knew who said. The difference between Isaac and Jacob is not that one asked and the other did not. Both asked. The difference is what each of them did with the answer, and that is this episode.

**Narrator** *(calm)* This episode is part of our series on sight lost and not given back: six people in the Bible who lost their sight and lived the rest of their lives without it. We tell them in three pairs. Two men blinded by their enemies, Zedekiah and Samson. A father and a son, Isaac and Jacob. Two old men of Shiloh, Eli and Ahijah. Every episode begins with sounds, and with what a blind man hears in them. Along the way we follow the Hebrew words the Bible uses for eyes that fail, one word handing on to the next. When we imagine a scene, we will tell you so. Everything else is from the text, from the old books read in their own voice, and from Brian.

**Narrator** *(calm)* First, where we are. Jacob left his father's tent with a stolen blessing and ran to Haran, and the Bible spends the rest of his life paying him back in his own coin. In Haran his uncle put the wrong sister into his bed in the dark, and in the morning there was Leah. Years later his own sons brought him a robe dipped in goat's blood, and he recognized it by touch and sight and believed his favourite son was dead. The man who fooled a blind father with a goatskin and a garment was fooled by the dark, and then by a goat and a garment. He was a hundred and thirty when he learned that Joseph was alive and ruling Egypt, and he went down to him and said, finally I can die, now that I have seen your face. He did not die. He lived seventeen more years in Goshen, and in those years his eyes went. By the chapter we are reading he is a hundred and forty-seven, he has made Joseph swear to carry him home to the family grave, and he is ill. Joseph is told, and he comes, and he brings his two sons.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Where this is. Stand in Goshen, in the green north-east of Egypt, where the Nile spreads out toward the sea, and face north, with north at twelve o'clock. Nothing from the rest of this series is near you. The land of the promise is a long way off at two o'clock, and three places in it are named in this one chapter. Hebron, where Abraham and Isaac lie in the cave Jacob has just made Joseph swear to carry him to, is about two weeks' walk at two o'clock. Bethlehem, where he buried Rachel beside the road, is a few hours beyond it on the same line. And Bethel, which he still calls Luz, where God appeared to him on his way out with nothing, is a day past that. Keep those three in mind. A blind man in Egypt is about to name every one of them from his bed.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Now as for me, when I was returning from Paddan, to my sorrow Rachel died along the way in the land of Canaan, some distance from Ephrath. So I buried her there beside the road to Ephrath” (that is, Bethlehem).

**Narrator** *(calm)* Luz, and the road to Bethlehem. He has not seen either in seventeen years and will not see them again, and he places them from his bed as exactly as a man who still walks them. Then, verse 8.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Israel saw the sons of Joseph, he asked, “Who are these?”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Joseph said to his father, “They are the sons God has given me in this place.” So Jacob said, “Please bring them to me, that I may bless them.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Now Israel’s eyesight was poor because of old age; he could hardly see. Joseph brought his sons to him, and his father kissed them and embraced them.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “I never expected to see your face again,” Israel said to Joseph, “but now God has let me see your children as well.”

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Two verses, and they seem to disagree. Verse 8 says Israel saw the sons of Joseph. Verse 10 says he could hardly see. Put them together and you have a man who can tell there are two shapes in the room and cannot tell whose they are. Many blind people live exactly there, and Brian will have something to say about it. So he does what Isaac did. He asks who they are, and he has them brought close, and he kisses them and holds them. The hands again. And then he says a thing that only a man in his condition would put that way: God has let me see your children. He cannot make out their faces. He is holding them. That is what seeing means to him now. Verses 12 to 20.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then Joseph removed his sons from his father’s knees and bowed facedown.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And Joseph took both of them—with Ephraim in his right hand toward Israel’s left hand, and Manasseh in his left hand toward Israel’s right hand—and brought them close to him.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But Israel stretched out his right hand and put it on the head of Ephraim, the younger; and crossing his hands, he put his left on Manasseh’s head, although Manasseh was the firstborn.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then he blessed Joseph and said: “May the God before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* the angel who has redeemed me from all harm—may He bless these boys. And may they be called by my name and the names of my fathers Abraham and Isaac, and may they grow into a multitude upon the earth.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Joseph saw that his father had placed his right hand on Ephraim’s head, he was displeased and took his father’s hand to move it from Ephraim’s head to Manasseh’s.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* “Not so, my father!” Joseph said. “This one is the firstborn; put your right hand on his head.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But his father refused. “I know, my son, I know!” he said. “He too shall become a people, and he too shall be great; nevertheless, his younger brother shall be greater than he, and his offspring shall become a multitude of nations.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* So that day Jacob blessed them and said: “By you shall Israel pronounce this blessing: ‘May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh.’” So he put Ephraim before Manasseh.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Count what Joseph did, because Joseph is the sighted man in the room and he did everything a sighted man does. He placed the boys by eye, the older to his father's right hand, the younger to his left, so that a blind man reaching straight out could not get it wrong. He watched the hands. When he saw them cross, he reached in and took hold of his father's hand to put it where it belonged. Every one of those is kindness. Every one of those is also the assumption that the man in the bed did not know what he was doing. And now count what Jacob did. He knew which boy was under which hand. He crossed his arms on purpose, and the Hebrew word for what he did with his hands means to act with understanding, the way the old translation has it, guiding his hands wittingly. And when the hand came in to correct him, he did not let it.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now imagine that one moment, the one the text gives in half a verse. Joseph took his father's hand to move it.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

*(SFX, if you can: the sickroom again. Very quiet. Two boys trying not to move.)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Jacob's hands are on two heads. He knows which is which, because he placed them, and because the one under his right hand is smaller. The blessing is half said. And then he feels a grip on his right wrist. Not a boy's hand. A man's, strong, careful, a hand that has signed for the grain of Egypt. He knows that hand. He has not felt it on him since it was a boy's hand, in Hebron, before the robe. It is lifting his hand off the smaller head, gently, the way you move an old man's hand off a thing he has got wrong.

**Joseph** *(low, firm)* Not so, my father. This one is the firstborn. Put your right hand on his head.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Israel** *(old, unhurried)* I know, my son. I know.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* I know. Seventy years earlier, in another room, a sighted son's hand went out to a blind father's and the blind father was fooled by it. This time the hand goes out to correct, not to deceive, and the blind father is not moved by it. He had not made a mistake. He had made a choice, and he had made it with the only thing anyone has ever been able to make that choice with, which is not eyes. Isaac blessed the wrong son by accident and let it stand. Jacob blessed the younger son on purpose and would not be talked out of it. The same family, the same hands, the same crossing of the older and the younger, and the second blind man is the one who gets it right.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, what do you hear in this one?

*(SFX, if you can: the sickroom from the opening, very quietly under his first line)*

**Brian** *(calm)* Joseph reached in to move his father's hand because he assumed that a man who could not see did not know what he was doing. Jacob's answer was two words, said twice: I know. The Hebrew verb there is yada, and the old dictionary says it means to know, properly to ascertain by seeing. Genesis gives the seeing-verb for knowing to a man who could hardly see, and it gives it to him about what his hands had told him. That is the whole page for me.

**Brian** *(calm)* Blind people are underestimated because sighted people tend to think sight is a vastly superior sense to all the others. I say touch is the most important sense. Think about how much of walking is the sense of touch through your feet, and how much of the rest of the day is your hands and your fingers. We move through this world by a combination of senses. When a fire truck comes along, you hear it long before you see it, and the hearing is what tells you to pull over. Our senses work together to get information to a brain that is locked away from the world behind a thick skull. Jacob's eyes had gone, and his hands, his memory and his judgment had not. He arranged the boys, crossed his arms on purpose, and when the sighted man in the room tried to correct him, he said what every blind person has had to say at some point: I know.

**Brian** *(calm)* Loss of sight does not take understanding with it. It does not take faith, or judgment, or memory, or kindness, or any of the many things that make a person who they are. The writer of Hebrews picked this scene, out of all of Jacob's long life, as his one act of faith: by faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of Joseph's sons. Dying, nearly blind, and that is the moment chosen.

**Brian** *(calm)* One more thing I noticed. Genesis does not use the same word for Isaac's sight loss and for Jacob's. Isaac's eyes were kahah, weak, grown dim. Jacob's were kabed, heavy, which is the Bible's word for a burden. I know both of those. Some days sight loss is a dimming you learn to live around. Some days it is a weight you carry. Jacob carried his to the end of the chapter and blessed twelve sons by name with it on him.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the old books. Matthew Henry noticed the same two things Brian did, that Jacob went blind the way his father did, and that the word is a heavy one. He put them in one breath.

**Henry** *(calm)* Those that have the honour of age must therewith be content to take the burden of it. The eye of faith may be very clear even when the eye of the body is very much clouded.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian put that second sentence on his page, because it is what the story is about. Henry goes on to the crossed hands, and he is careful about what they were not. Jacob did not cross them by mistake, he says, nor in a humour, nor from a partial affection to one boy more than the other, but from a spirit of prophecy, in compliance with the divine counsels. Then he makes a list, and it is worth hearing, because Jacob is on it twice. God, he says, has so often advanced the younger above the elder: Abel above Cain, Isaac above Ishmael, Jacob above Esau, Ephraim above Manasseh, David above his elder brothers. He tied the Jews to observe the birthright, Henry says, but he never tied himself to observe it. Which means the crossed hands were not a blind man's confusion. They were the family pattern, and the one man in the room who could not see was the one who could see it.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

---

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the word. Each of these six stories has its own word for eyes that fail, and they make a chain. Zedekiah's word was "ah-VAR," to blind, a thing done to a man. Isaac's was "kah-HAH," to grow dim. Jacob's is a different word again: "kah-VED." Heavy. Verse 10 says, in the Hebrew, that Israel's eyes were heavy from age. It is one of the commonest words in the Bible, and it is almost never about eyes. It is the word for Pharaoh's heart when he hardened it, heavy. It is the word for the people's ears in Isaiah when they would not hear, heavy. And with a slightly different vowel it is kavod, glory, the weight of God's presence. The same root carries the hardest hearts and the glory of God, and once, here, an old man's eyes.

**Narrator** *(calm)* So the chain so far runs: a king's eyes put out, a father's eyes grown dim, a son's eyes grown heavy. Two of those happened the ordinary way, by age, to two men in one family, and Genesis gave each of them his own word. Brian hears the difference between them, a dimming and a weight, and the Bible seems to as well. Next time the chain goes back to Isaac's word, dim, and adds one more, for an old priest whose eyes had stopped moving.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* What came after. Jacob was not finished. The next chapter opens with him calling all twelve of his sons to the bed, and it ends with a sentence that is easy to read past. Genesis chapter 49, verse 28.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* These are the tribes of Israel, twelve in all, and this was what their father said to them. He blessed them, and he blessed each one with a suitable blessing.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Each one with a suitable blessing. Twelve men, in order, by name, with a word fitted to each, from a man who could not see which of them was standing where. He would have known them by their voices and their places, the way Isaac should have known Jacob. Then he gave the instructions about the cave one more time, and verse 33.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* When Jacob had finished instructing his sons, he pulled his feet into the bed and breathed his last, and he was gathered to his people.

**Narrator** *(calm)* His sons did as he had charged them. They carried him up out of Egypt, two weeks' walk at two o'clock, and buried him in the cave at Machpelah with Abraham and Isaac. The blind father and the blind son, in one cave. And the New Testament, in the chapter that lists the people who lived by faith, gives the father and the son two verses, back to back. Hebrews chapter 11, verses 20 and 21.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau concerning the future.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* By faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of Joseph’s sons and worshiped as he leaned on the top of his staff.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Two blind men, two blessings, and the Bible's one word for both of them is faith. Isaac's came by faith after his hands had failed him. Jacob's came by faith with his hands doing exactly what he meant them to. And leaning on the top of his staff, which is the one detail Hebrews adds. He was holding on to something while he did it. Blind men usually are.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, with the last word.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. Every blind person I know has had a hand reach in to move theirs. Usually it is kind. Usually the person is sure they are helping. And the hardest two words to say in that moment, without heat, are the ones Jacob said. I know. He did not argue, and he did not explain himself to Joseph until after. He just did not let the hand move his, and he finished the blessing. He had learned in a tent in Beersheba what hands can be made to say, and he spent the rest of his life finding out that eyes are no better. At the end, with both gone, he still knew who was under each hand, and he still knew what God had said. That is what he was leaning on.

The old priest in the bed cannot see the lamp. He is the one who knows whose voice it is. Eli, and the voice in the night.
