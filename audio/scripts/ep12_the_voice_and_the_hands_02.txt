[BRIAN] (calm) DRAFT. I hear trust. This chapter brings home the reality of the trust we place in the people around us, especially as the other senses start to fail. Isaac did everything right. He asked, he listened, he reached out, he smelled the clothes. And the people he trusted had prepared for every one of those. When I first read this, my thought was that Isaac's sense of touch must have failed along with his sight, because how else could goatskin pass for a man's hands? I have changed my mind, and the reason is my own hands. Mine sit in conditioned air all day on a keyboard. My wife Barb says they are soft as a baby's bottom. Isaac's were not. He had spent a hundred years outdoors among flocks and tents and wells, doing hard work for meagre provision. Hands like that do not feel fine detail the way mine do. Josephus says the twins differed only in their hairiness. Put a goatskin under a hand like Isaac's and it is not the stretch it sounds. It helps to remember where he was. Beersheba was his place. He had reopened his father's well there and named it, and he was still there decades later when Jacob left. A blind man in a camp he has known for half a lifetime is a different man from one set down in a strange room. He would have known his tent, his way to the door, who usually came and went. That is part of why the deceit worked. Nothing about the day was strange except the one thing that mattered. The detail that stays with me is Esau's voice. A few minutes earlier Isaac had picked out Jacob's voice through a disguise, so his hearing was fine. Yet when Esau walked in, Isaac asked, "Who are you?" Nothing was wrong with his ears. What had changed was what he expected. He had just blessed Esau and sent him out, so the man coming in could not be Esau. I call this context. My ears tell me a great deal, but what I expect to hear tells me almost as much, and when the two disagree, expectation often wins. It won twice in one hour for Isaac. DRAFT. People ask me how I can trust anyone, living like this. The honest answer is that I have no choice, and neither did Isaac, and neither do you. You just do not notice, because your eyes let you pretend you checked. I check with everything I have, the way he did, and then I take the word of the person in the room. Most days that is enough. This chapter is the Bible's record of the day it was not, and I notice that the Bible does not blame the blind man for it.

[BOOKS] (calm) The intelligent use made by Isaac of his hearing, touch, and smell, which is the more surprising as it might naturally be supposed that by reason of age these senses would have become almost useless.
[NARRATOR] (calm) And at the end of the passage he adds that the sagacity Isaac showed would have been almost enough, under ordinary circumstances, to find the fraud out. A blind man read it and saw a careful blind man. Matthew Henry, a century and a half earlier, read it as a sighted man, and he went sense by sense through what Rebekah did: his taste, with kid dressed as venison; his touch and his smell, with the clothes and the skins. [NARRATOR] (calm) Now the word. Each of these six stories has its own word for eyes that fail, and they make a chain. Last time the word was "ah-VAR," to blind, a thing done to a man. This time it is a thing that happens to him: "kah-HAH." To grow dim, to grow dull, to fade. Genesis 27 opens with it: his eyes were dim from seeing. It is the ordinary word for old eyes going, and that is what makes it worth following, because the Bible uses it twice more of old men, and once to say it did not happen. The second old man is Eli, at Shiloh, two episodes from now: his eyes had begun to grow dim, the same word, so that he could not see. The third is in the book of Job, where Job says his eye has grown dim from grief, the same word used of sorrow instead of years. And the one denial is Moses. Deuteronomy says that at a hundred and twenty his eye was not dim, this exact word with a not in front of it, and his strength had not left him. The Bible knows the difference between an old man whose eyes went and an old man whose eyes did not, and it uses one word for both. Isaac is the first in the chain who simply grew old. Zedekiah's blindness was done to him. Isaac's came the way it comes to most people, and the chapter never treats it as a judgment. It is just the fact the whole family built their plan around.
