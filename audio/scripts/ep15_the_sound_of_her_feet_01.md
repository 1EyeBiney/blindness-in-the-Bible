**Narrator** *(calm)* Imagine a house in Shiloh, a long time ago. The old man inside cannot see. Someone has just stopped at his door, and has not said anything yet.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Ahijah** *(old, not loud, certain)* Come in, wife of Jeroboam. Why are you disguised?

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* She has not spoken. He has not seen her. She is the queen of Israel, dressed as a village woman with a basket, and the first word out of the house is her name. Here is Brian, by way of Mister Scaredy Cat, on what a blind man hears at a door.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. I can tell Barb's sound if she wears certain shoes, but often, in places without context, it is hard to pick out her footsteps among others. So I want to be honest about this before we start. Ahijah did not recognize the sound of her feet. Nothing in the text says he did, and I would not have. What he recognized was the moment. He had been told who was coming, and that she would be dressed as someone else, and when feet stopped at the door at the right time, he knew. There is a difference between hearing who someone is and being told, and this is the one story in the series where the blind man gets the second, and does not need the first.

**Narrator** *(calm)* This episode is part of our series on sight lost and not given back: six people in the Bible who lost their sight and lived the rest of their lives without it. We tell them in three pairs. Two men blinded by their enemies, Zedekiah and Samson. A father and a son, Isaac and Jacob. Two old men of Shiloh, Eli and Ahijah. **Narrator** *(calm)* First, where we are. Solomon is dead and the kingdom has split in two. Years before that, when Jeroboam was only one of Solomon's officers, a prophet from Shiloh named Ahijah met him alone on the road out of Jerusalem, took hold of the new cloak he was wearing, and tore it into twelve pieces. First Kings chapter 11, verses 29 to 31, and then 38.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* During that time, the prophet Ahijah the Shilonite met Jeroboam on the road as he was going out of Jerusalem. Now Ahijah had wrapped himself in a new cloak, and the two of them were alone in the open field.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And Ahijah took hold of the new cloak he was wearing, tore it into twelve pieces,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* and said to Jeroboam, “Take ten pieces for yourself, for this is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: ‘Behold, I will tear the kingdom out of the hand of Solomon, and I will give you ten tribes.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* If you listen to all that I command you, walk in My ways, and do what is right in My sight in order to keep My statutes and commandments as My servant David did, then I will be with you. I will build you a lasting dynasty just as I built for David, and I will give Israel to you.

**Narrator** *(calm)* It came true, the first half of it. Ten tribes made Jeroboam king. The second half had a condition on it, and instead Jeroboam was afraid his people would go up to Jerusalem to worship and their hearts would go back to David's line, so he made two golden calves, set one at Bethel and one at Dan, built shrines, and made priests who were not Levites. He never sent to Ahijah about any of that. Then his son Abijah fell ill. The boy was the heir, and he was, the text will say, the only good thing in the house. And now Jeroboam sends to Shiloh. The prophet is old, and the text says plainly that he cannot see.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Where this is. Stand at Shiloh again, where we stood last time, and face north, with north at twelve o'clock. Eli's gate is somewhere near you, and the house of the LORD is long gone from here. The queen comes from Tirzah, Jeroboam's city, about a day's walk at one o'clock, and that is the road the footsteps are on. Bethel, where one of the golden calves stands, is half a day behind you at seven o'clock. And a day's walk at six o'clock is Jerusalem, with Solomon's Temple in it, the house the king has forbidden his people to go up to. The queen's road comes down from the north and ends at a blind man's door.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* First Kings chapter 14, verses 1 to 6.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* At that time Abijah son of Jeroboam became ill,

> **Reader** *(plainly)* and Jeroboam said to his wife, “Now get up, disguise yourself so they will not recognize you as my wife, and go to Shiloh. For Ahijah the prophet is there; it was he who spoke about my kingship over this people.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Take with you ten loaves of bread, some cakes, and a jar of honey, and go to him. He will tell you what will become of the boy.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Jeroboam’s wife did as instructed; she arose and went to Shiloh and arrived at Ahijah’s house. Now Ahijah could not see, for his eyes were dim because of his age.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* But the LORD had said to Ahijah, “Behold, the wife of Jeroboam is coming to ask you about her son, for he is ill. You are to say such and such to her, because when she arrives, she will be disguised.”

> **Reader** *(plainly)* So when Ahijah heard the sound of her feet entering the door, he said, “Come in, wife of Jeroboam! Why are you disguised? For I have been sent to you with bad news.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Notice who the disguise is for. Jeroboam says it himself: so they will not recognize you. They. The people on the road and in the town. A king's wife walking into a village that used to be the holy place, asking a prophet about the king's heir, would be talked about by nightfall, and he did not want the talk. The disguise was never aimed at the prophet. It was aimed at eyes, and the one man it was carried to had none. Then notice the order of the verses. Verse 4 says Ahijah could not see. Verse 5 says the LORD had already told him who was coming and how she would be dressed. Verse 6 says he heard her feet and named her. The text puts the blindness, the telling, and the hearing in a row, so that you cannot miss which one did the work. He did not see through her. He was told.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now imagine the walk, because the text gives us the frame and not the road. She arose and went to Shiloh. A day, from Tirzah, mostly uphill.

*(pause 1.0 s)*

*(SFX, if you can: a hill road at morning. Footsteps, a basket creaking, a distant flock. Under the next lines.)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* She has done this carefully. A plain cloak, the kind a farmer's wife wears to market. On her arm a basket with the gift a poor woman brings a holy man: ten loaves, some cakes, a jar of honey. Nothing a queen would carry. She passes people on the road and no one looks twice, which is the point. All day she is a woman nobody recognizes, and all day the thing she is afraid of is a face: someone from the court, someone from the old days, someone who will say her name. She reaches Shiloh. She asks for the prophet's house and is pointed to it. She goes up the path, and stops at the door, and the first thing she sees in the dim room is the old man's face, and his eyes. They are open. They are not moving. They are not looking at her, or at anything. And in that half second, before he says a word, she knows that everything she did to her face this morning was wasted on the one man she did it for.

*(SFX, required: her footsteps on the path, the last few, and stopping.)*

**Ahijah** *(old, not unkind)* Come in, wife of Jeroboam. Why are you disguised? For I have been sent to you with bad news.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Sent. He does not say I knew you, or I heard you. He says I have been sent to you. And then he gives her the message as it was given to him, for her husband. Verses 7 to 9.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Go, tell Jeroboam that this is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: ‘I raised you up from among the people and appointed you ruler over My people Israel.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* I tore the kingdom away from the house of David and gave it to you. But you have not been like My servant David, who kept My commandments and followed Me with all his heart, doing only what was right in My eyes.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* You have done more evil than all who came before you. You have proceeded to make for yourself other gods and molten images to provoke Me, and you have flung Me behind your back.

**Narrator** *(calm)* The next two verses are the end of Jeroboam's house, in the plainest terms the Bible has for such things, and we will leave them on the page. Then the prophet turns from the king to the woman at the door, and to the reason she came. Verses 12 and 13, and then 15, because it reaches a long way.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* As for you, get up and go home. When your feet enter the city, the child will die.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* All Israel will mourn for him and bury him. For this is the only one belonging to Jeroboam who will receive a proper burial, because only in him has the LORD, the God of Israel, found any good in the house of Jeroboam.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* For the LORD will strike Israel as a reed is shaken in the water. He will uproot Israel from this good land that He gave their fathers, and He will scatter them beyond the Euphrates, because they have made their Asherah poles, provoking the LORD to anger.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Beyond the Euphrates. That is the first time in the Bible anyone says it. Two hundred years before it happened to the north, and three hundred and fifty before Zedekiah was marched down that river, a blind man in Shiloh said the word exile to a woman with a basket, and the map we drew in the first episode of this series was already in his mouth.

**Narrator** *(calm)* When your feet enter the city. Her feet again. The chapter is built on them: the king tells her to get up and go, she gets up and goes, the blind man hears her feet at his door, and the word he gives her is that when those same feet cross into Tirzah her boy will die. She asked nothing. The text never gives her a word. She came to a man who could not see her to find out whether her son would live, and he told her, and he told her more than she asked, and the only tenderness in it is the last line. The boy would be mourned, and buried, and he alone, because in him alone the LORD had found something good.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, what do you hear in this one?

*(SFX, if you can: the path and the morning from the opening, very quietly under his first line)*

**Brian** *(calm)* When I started on this story I thought it would be the one where I talked about our other senses, and how much people underestimate what a blind man hears. The text turned that around on me. Ahijah did not hear who she was. He did not work it out from anything. The LORD told him before she arrived who was coming and that she would be disguised, and when he heard feet at the door he said her name. That is how he knew. Not sharper ears. He was told.

**Brian** *(calm)* The word for his eyes is the same one used for Eli at the gate in the same town: his eyes stood. Set. Still. The translators cannot settle on it, dim in one verse and set or fixed in the other, but the picture is plain enough to me. Eyes that are still are eyes that are not tracking anything, and are not seeing much or anything at all. I am getting more this way the longer I am blind. I do not use my eyes for anything now, and they have stopped moving the way eyes do when they are looking. It is the one description of blindness in this series written from the outside, of what another person sees when they look at a blind man's face. The queen saw that face when she walked in, and whatever her husband had told her, she would have known then that the disguise was wasted on him.

**Brian** *(calm)* This story also finishes something that starts back in Genesis. Isaac could not tell Jacob from Esau. Jacob could not tell Leah from Rachel in the dark. Joseph's brothers stood in front of him in broad daylight and did not recognize him. All through that family the question is whether a man can tell who is in front of him, and his eyes, when he has them, are no help. The queen came to Shiloh disguised so that nobody would recognize her, and the text never says the prophet did. It says he was told. He is the first one in the whole line who did not have to recognize anybody, and that is the point of him.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. I did not expect the blind man in this story to be the one who was not distracted. Jeroboam had his eyes and his kingdom and his calves, and he never once sent to Ahijah to ask about any of that. He sent when his boy was sick. My lack of vision gives me clarity in a great many areas. I am less distracted, in a world that is built to pull my attention away from Jesus, where it needs to be. The old man in Shiloh had nothing to look at, and he was the one person in the story who could see what was going on, because he was listening to the only voice that knew.

*(pause 2.0 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the old books. Matthew Henry wrote one sentence about Ahijah that Brian did not expect to agree with, and does.

**Henry** *(calm)* He sent to Ahijah the prophet, who lived obscurely and neglected in Shiloh, blind through age, yet still blest with the visions of the Almighty, which need not bodily eyes, but are rather favoured by the want of them, the eyes of the mind being then most intent and least diverted.

**Narrator** *(calm)* Least diverted. Henry also put the king's foolishness in one question. Could the God of Israel see into the thick darkness of the future, he asks, and not see through the thin veil of this disguise? And he noticed what Jeroboam had not done. He had not sent to Ahijah for advice about setting up his calves, or consecrating his priests, but had recourse to him in his distress, when the gods he served could give him no relief. Henry puts it in a line Brian kept: in trouble have those visited thee who before slighted thee. Josephus tells the story the same way as the text, with one addition that is really just the text said twice. When she came in, he has Ahijah say to her, you are not hidden from God, who appeared to me and told me you were coming.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Now the word. Each of these six stories has its own word for eyes that fail, and they make a chain. Zedekiah's was "ah-VAR," blinded. Isaac's was "kah-HAH," dim. Jacob's was "kah-VED," heavy. Eli had Isaac's word in the night and a new one at the gate, and Ahijah has only that last one. His eyes "KOOM," stood. The same word as Eli's, the same town, and nowhere else in the Bible are eyes described this way. It is the ordinary verb for standing up, and this chapter is full of it. Jeroboam tells his wife to get up and go: that is the word. She arose and went: the word. Ahijah tells her, get up and go home: the word again. Everyone in the chapter is rising and going, and in the middle of them is one man whose eyes have risen and stopped. The queen's feet went from Tirzah to Shiloh and back. His eyes went nowhere. He knew more than anyone on the road.

**Narrator** *(calm)* So the chain stands at five, with one to go. A king blinded, a father dimmed, a son weighed down, two old men of one town whose eyes went dim and then stood still. Five men, and not one of them got his sight back, and not one of them is described as less than he was. Next time the last word, and it is the hardest one, and it closes the loop back to where we began.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* What came after. Verses 17 and 18.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* Then Jeroboam’s wife got up and departed for Tirzah, and as soon as she stepped over the threshold of the house, the boy died.

> **Reader** *(plainly)* And they buried him, and all Israel mourned for him, according to the word that the LORD had spoken through His servant Ahijah the prophet.

**Narrator** *(calm)* The threshold of the house. Not Ahijah's. Her own, in Tirzah, a day later, the word keeping its time to the step. And the rest of it came as well. Jeroboam reigned twenty-two years and died, his son reigned two, and a man named Baasha killed him and every person left of Jeroboam's house, according, the text says, to the word that the LORD had spoken through His servant Ahijah the Shilonite. That is the last time his name appears. An old blind man in a town the glory had left, who never went to the court and whom the court came to twice, once for a kingdom and once for a child, and who said both times exactly what he was given.

*(pause 1.5 s)*

**Narrator** *(calm)* Brian, with the last word.

**Brian** *(calm)* DRAFT. People assume a blind man is the easiest person in the room to fool, and some days they are right. The queen's husband assumed it too. What Ahijah had was not better ears than hers or sharper wits than his. He had been told, by the one voice that is never wrong about who is at the door, and he believed it before her feet reached the step. That is all that any of us has. I check what I can, and I am wrong about footsteps all the time. But I am not walking by what I can check.
