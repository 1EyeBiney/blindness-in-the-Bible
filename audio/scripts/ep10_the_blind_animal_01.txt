[NARRATOR] (calm) First, where we are. Three places and a thousand years. Leviticus chapter twenty-two, at Sinai, gives the rule for what may be offered. Deuteronomy chapter fifteen, on the plains east of the Jordan forty years later, applies it to the firstborn of the flock, which belonged to God. And Malachi, the last book of the Old Testament, speaks in Jerusalem a thousand years after Sinai, to priests of the second Temple who were offering blind, lame and sick animals and calling the LORD's table contemptible.

[READER] (plainly) You must not present anything with a defect, because it will not be accepted on your behalf.
[READER] (plainly) When a man presents a peace offering to the LORD from the herd or flock to fulfill a vow or as a freewill offering, it must be without blemish or defect to be acceptable.
[READER] (plainly) You are not to present to the LORD any animal that is blind, injured, or maimed, or anything with a running sore, a festering rash, or a scab; you must not put any of these on the altar as a food offering to the LORD.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Deuteronomy chapter 15, verses 19 to 23.
[READER] (plainly) You must set apart to the LORD your God every firstborn male produced by your herds and flocks. You are not to put the firstborn of your oxen to work, nor are you to shear the firstborn of your flock.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the words, and this law has two, a negative and a positive. The negative is one you heard last time. The animal must not have a mum, a blemish. It is exactly the word of the blind priest. The priest with a mum does not approach; the lamb with a mum is not accepted. The Law used the same measure for the man and for the lamb, and in the end, as the encyclopedia notes, for Christ, the lamb without blemish.
[NARRATOR] (calm) The positive word is tamim. Tah-meem. The animal must be tamim, which the Berean text gives as unblemished and Strong's as entire, whole, complete. It is the Passover lamb's word.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Exodus chapter 12, verse 5.
[READER] (plainly) Your lamb must be an unblemished year-old male, and you may take it from the sheep or the goats.
[NARRATOR] (calm) But here is what the dictionary cannot tell you and the Bible can. Tamim is a word used of people far more often than of animals. Listen.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Deuteronomy chapter 18, verse 13.
[READER] (plainly) You must be blameless before the LORD your God.
[NARRATOR] (beat) Blameless. Tamim.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Psalm chapter 15, verse 2.
[READER] (plainly) He who walks with integrity and practices righteousness, who speaks the truth from his heart,
[NARRATOR] (beat) With integrity. Tamim.
[NARRATOR] (calm) So the thing the altar asked of a lamb is the thing God asks of a person's walk. Whole. And in neither case does it have anything to do with eyes. A blind man can walk tamim. That is the whole of the Law's answer to the word defect, and it is the beginning of the answer to Brian's word, broken.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the old books, and a connection you may not expect. This law is why there was a market in the Temple. Here is Edersheim, writing in eighteen eighty-three, on what happened when a worshipper arrived with an animal.
[BOOKS] (calm) When an animal was brought, it had to be examined as to its Levitical fitness by persons regularly qualified and appointed.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Qualified examiners, who charged a fee. And so, Edersheim says, a shortcut appeared.
[BOOKS] (calm) All trouble and difficulty would be avoided by a regular market within the Temple-enclosure, where sacrificial animals could be purchased, having presumably been duly inspected, and all fees paid before being offered for sale.
[BOOKS] (calm) Most improper transactions were carried on, to the taking undue advantage of the poor people who came to offer their sacrifices.
[NARRATOR] (calm) He gives a case from the rabbis of a pair of pigeons run up to the price of a gold coin. Now think back to episode five. The tables Jesus overturned in the Temple court, on the day the blind and the lame came to Him there, were this market. The rule that God be given the best had become a trade that took the most from the poor. The law was good. What people built on it was not.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Brian, on the word broken.
[BRIAN] (calm) This one was harder to read than I expected, and not because of what it asks of a lamb. It is because it puts blindness straight into the category of broken. In society's terms I am disabled. But when I hear of a disabled car, I can swap in the word broken without losing anything, and I most certainly do not feel broken. I do most tasks differently now, with a different set of skills and more steps. Yet I still carry nearly all the same obligations, responsibilities, enjoyments and pleasures that I had with full sight.
[BRIAN] (calm) If anything, I am better at the things God wants me to be better at. Fewer distractions means more focused attention on the people I love, and on the people I need to go and find to love.
[BRIAN] (calm) The law itself leaves me a door. The blind firstborn was not thrown away. Deuteronomy says to eat it at home, as you would a gazelle or a deer, and the page above says what that means: not fit for the altar is not the same as worthless. A thing with a defect still fed a family. I would not have chosen the word defect for myself. But I notice that even this law, the hardest one on these pages for me to read, does not call the blind animal broken. It calls it ordinary.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) One last word, and it is the one we planted in the first episode. Malachi ends his charge with, My name is to be feared among the nations. That is yare. Yah-ray. To fear, or to revere. It is the word of the stumbling block law: you shall fear your God. The first law about the blind and the last both rest on it. And look at why. Nobody would see the stone placed in the road. Nobody at the altar could tell, once the animal was offered, whether a better one had been kept back at home. Both laws were written for the wrong that no one catches, and both give the same reason to keep them. God saw.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Look back over the four. Mikshol, the stumbling block: a word about the fall, not the trip, and a law written for the people who do not catch themselves. Ayin and chophshi, the eye and the freedom: the one price the Law ever put on an eye, and it was paid to the man who lost it, in the coin of the seventh year. Mum and tamim, the blemish and the whole: one measure for the priest and the lamb, and a word for wholeness that a blind man can walk in as well as anyone. And yare, the fear of God, at the beginning and the end.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Four laws. Not one of them calls a blind person broken. One protects him by name. One frees him. One keeps him in the priesthood and at the table. And one, about lambs, says that God is given the best, and then lets the blind animal feed a family. Read as a whole, the Law's word on the blind is not a word about what they lack. It is a word to everyone else, about how to treat them, and about who is watching.
