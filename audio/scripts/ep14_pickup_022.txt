[BRIAN] {id=022} (calm) Eli is the one in this series I would least like to be. Not because of how he went blind, which was slowly, with age, the way most people do. Because of the last scene. He is ninety-eight, he cannot see, and he is sitting in a chair by the road waiting for news that everyone around him will get before he does. The text says his heart trembled for the ark. He could do nothing about the battle, nothing about his sons, nothing but sit there and listen for it. That is the part of blindness that nobody warns you about: the patience you have to have whether you want it or not. Sighted people choose when to be patient. A blind person waits for the ride, waits for the person who said they would lend their eyeballs, waits to be told what everybody else in the room already knows. The city cried out before anyone told Eli why, and he had to ask. Why this commotion? I have asked that question, in those words or near enough, more times than I can count. Something has happened, the room knows it, and you are the last to be told, and usually the telling is the gentle version. Eli got the straight version, and the last thing in it was the ark. Quite often, the frustration is not the waiting itself. It is not being able to do anything with what you hear. Eli had heard for years what his sons were doing. The text says so: I hear about your wicked deeds from all these people. He spoke to them and they would not listen, and the man of God came, and then the boy came in the night with the same word again. A blind man hears a great deal he cannot act on. I know the sound of a problem starting long before I can get to it, and getting to it is the whole difficulty. There are all kinds of things I hear that people think I do not. Usually it is when a person does not want to share. My wife Barb has a disability of her own, and it puts limits on a heart that was made for serving people. Some nights I hear her crying softly behind a door in another room, over what she can no longer do for others. Jimmy, who lives with us, spent years doing God's work and should be enjoying a peaceful retirement. Instead he has a job that wears his body down, and when he thinks the house is asleep I hear the muffled grunts of a tired man in pain. Neither of them is telling me. I hear it anyway. A sighted person can look away from something and believe it has not been seen. There is no looking away from a sound. So you carry what you heard, and you decide whether to say so, and that is its own kind of patience.
