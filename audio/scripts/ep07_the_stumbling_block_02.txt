# Not By Sight, episode 7: The stumbling block. Draft script. First of four on the Law.
# BOOKS reads the old commentators and the other law codes, word for word. No imagined scenes in this series.

[NARRATOR] (calm) Before the law, a word about words.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Most words carry more than one meaning, and we choose between them without noticing. If someone tells you a man fell, you do not yet know whether he lost his footing or lost his life. If someone says a door is open, it may be unlocked, or it may be standing wide. Usually the sentence around the word tells us which, and we move on. But sometimes the sentence does not tell us. Sometimes a word is doing more work than we can see, and the only way to find out is to follow it: to go where else it is used, and listen to what it means there.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (calm) That is what this series does. Four laws, and in each one a word that could mean more than one thing. We will read the law, stop at the word, and follow it through the rest of the Bible until it settles. Then we will know what God said about the blind, because we will know what the words meant to the people who first heard them.
[PAUSE 1.5]
[NARRATOR] (calm) One sentence of law. Thirty-one words in English, given at Sinai, more than three thousand years ago.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (plainly) You must not curse the deaf or place a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God. I am the LORD.
[PAUSE 1.5]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Brian read that sentence and stopped at one word. And now, Mister Scaredy Cat. Not Brian's voice, but his words.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[BRIAN] (calm) For me, a stumbling block is an annoyance more than anything: something I would stumble over, but not necessarily fall down. I stumble over curbs and parking stops all the time and somehow always seem to catch myself. I am fortunate to have enough of a sense of balance and spatial awareness for this to work. Most blind people I know will easily trip over curbs and parking stops, usually resulting in serious pain of some sort. So is this notion of a stumbling block more like me tripping slightly, or more like a blind person falling down and breaking or spraining something?
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (calm) That is the question this episode answers. A trip, or a fall. The answer is in the word itself, and in where else the Bible uses it.
[PAUSE 1.5]
[NARRATOR] (calm) This is Not By Sight, a series on blindness in the Bible, made by a blind Christian for blind believers, their families, and the church. The Scripture you will hear is the Berean Standard Bible, read word for word. The thoughts marked as Brian's are Brian Clark's own. And when we imagine a scene, we will tell you so. This episode is part of our series on the Law: what God commanded about the blind. In these episodes there are no imagined scenes. Everything you hear is from the text, from the old books read in their own voice, from the Hebrew words themselves, and from Brian.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) First, where we are. Leviticus chapter nineteen is a chapter of commands about ordinary life, given to the whole assembly of Israel at Mount Sinai, in the first year after they came out of Egypt. The verses around our verse are about leaving gleanings for the poor, paying a hired man on time, honest courts, and loving your neighbor as yourself. The law about the blind sits in the middle of them, between wages and justice. Where this is. Sinai lies far to the south of everything else in this series, off the bottom edge of our map. From Jerusalem it is about eleven days' walk, down through the desert. The second passage we will hear was spoken by Moses on the plains east of the Jordan, forty years later, and it looks forward to a place inside the land: two mountains facing each other across a narrow valley, with the town of Shechem between them. Stand in Jerusalem and face north, with north at twelve o'clock. Shechem is at twelve o'clock, about a day and a half's walk, in the hill country that would become Samaria. Hear the law in its place. Leviticus chapter 19, verses 13 to 18.
[READER] (plainly) You must not defraud your neighbor or rob him. You must not withhold until morning the wages due a hired hand. You must not curse the deaf or place a stumbling block before the blind, but you shall fear your God. I am the LORD. You must not pervert justice; you must not show partiality to the poor or favoritism to the rich; you are to judge your neighbor fairly. You must not go about spreading slander among your people. You must not endanger the life of your neighbor. I am the LORD. You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Directly rebuke your neighbor, so that you will not incur guilt on account of him. Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Notice the company it keeps. Wages. Fair courts. Slander. Grudges. Love your neighbor. The blind man and the deaf man are not set apart in a chapter of their own. They are in the chapter about how to treat the people next to you. Now the second passage. Moses is commanding a ceremony for the day Israel crosses the Jordan. Six tribes are to stand on one mountain and six on the other, and the Levites are to call out twelve curses, and the whole nation is to answer each one. The blind man is in the list. Deuteronomy chapter 27, verses 11 to 19.
[READER] (plainly) On that day Moses commanded the people: “When you have crossed the Jordan, these tribes shall stand on Mount Gerizim to bless the people: Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Joseph, and Benjamin. And these tribes shall stand on Mount Ebal to deliver the curse: Reuben, Gad, Asher, Zebulun, Dan, and Naphtali. Then the Levites shall proclaim in a loud voice to every Israelite: ‘Cursed is the man who makes a carved idol or molten image—an abomination to the LORD, the work of the hands of a craftsman—and sets it up in secret.’ And let all the people say, ‘Amen!’ ‘Cursed is he who dishonors his father or mother.’ And let all the people say, ‘Amen!’ ‘Cursed is he who moves his neighbor’s boundary stone.’ And let all the people say, ‘Amen!’ ‘Cursed is he who lets a blind man wander in the road.’ And let all the people say, ‘Amen!’ ‘Cursed is he who withholds justice from the foreigner, the fatherless, or the widow.’ And let all the people say, ‘Amen!’
[NARRATOR] (calm) Think about the sound of that. A valley with a mountain on either side, and on the slopes, a whole people. The Levites call out, and the answer comes back from both sides at once. Amen. Twelve times. And one of the twelve is for the man who lets a blind traveller wander off the road. Look at who his neighbours are in that list: the man who makes an idol in secret, the man who moves a boundary stone, the man who cheats the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow. Hidden wrongs, every one. Wrongs that no court would ever hear about.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the word. The Hebrew behind stumbling block is mikshol. Say it with me: mik-shole. Strong's dictionary, from 1894, gives it as a stumbling-block, literally or figuratively, and derives it from a verb that means to totter or fall. The 1915 encyclopedia says its literal sense is an object which causes one to stumble or fall, and it says something else that matters for Brian's question. Leviticus nineteen fourteen, it says, is the one place in the Bible where the word is plainly literal. Everywhere else it is used for what brings material ruin or spiritual downfall. So let us go everywhere else. Here is the same word, mikshol, in four other places. Listen to what happens to the people in them. Jeremiah chapter 6, verse 21.
[READER] (plainly) Therefore this is what the LORD says: “I will lay stumbling blocks before this people; fathers and sons alike will be staggered; friends and neighbors will perish.”
[NARRATOR] (beat) Friends and neighbors will perish. Ezekiel chapter 3, verse 20.
[READER] (plainly) Now if a righteous man turns from his righteousness and commits iniquity, and I put a stumbling block before him, he will die. If you did not warn him, he will die in his sin, and the righteous acts he did will not be remembered. And I will hold you responsible for his blood.
[NARRATOR] (beat) He will die. Isaiah chapter 8, verses 14 to 15.
[READER] (plainly) And He will be a sanctuary—but to both houses of Israel a stone of stumbling and a rock of offense, to the dwellers of Jerusalem a trap and a snare. Many will stumble over these; they will fall and be broken; they will be ensnared and captured.”
[NARRATOR] (beat) They will fall and be broken. Psalm chapter 119, verse 165.
[READER] (plainly) Abundant peace belongs to those who love Your law; nothing can make them stumble.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Only the psalm is gentle, and there the stumbling block is the thing that cannot happen to those who love God's law. Perish. Die. Fall and be broken. That is the company this word keeps. So here is the answer to Brian's question, on the words alone. The law is about the fall, not the trip. And in the word's other uses, about worse than a fall. It was written for the people who do not catch themselves.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Two more words from the same verse. The blind man is ivver, the plain word for blind, the one you will hear in all four of these episodes. And the reason the law gives, you shall fear your God, uses yare. Yah-ray. To fear, or to revere. Hold on to that word. It opens the Law's care for the blind here, and in the fourth episode you will hear it close the Law's last word on the subject, a thousand years later. And the curse in Deuteronomy uses a different verb for what is done to the blind man. He is made to wander: shagah, to stray, or to be led astray. The word for going wrong without knowing it.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Now the old books. In these episodes they speak for themselves, in a voice of their own, and I will tell you who is speaking and when. First, Matthew Henry, a pastor writing in England in seventeen hundred and six, on this verse.
[BOOKS] (calm) The safety of the blind we must likewise be tender of, and not put a stumbling-block before them; for this is to add affliction to the afflicted, and to make God's providence a servant to our malice. This prohibition implies a precept to help the blind, and remove stumbling-blocks out of their way.
[NARRATOR] (calm) And on the words, you shall fear your God, Henry gives the reason that still holds.
[BOOKS] (calm) Thou dost not fear the deaf and blind, they cannot right themselves; but remember it is the glory of God to help the helpless, and he will plead their cause.
[NARRATOR] (calm) That is the heart of it. Both wrongs in this verse are wrongs the victim cannot catch. The deaf man does not hear the curse. The blind man does not see who moved the stone, or who stood and watched him take the wrong road. No witness is coming forward. So the law does not appeal to a court. It appeals to the one who saw. Now Josephus, a Jewish historian writing for Roman readers about the year ninety-three, retelling the Law in everyday terms.
[BOOKS] (calm) It is also a duty to show the roads to those who do not know them, and not to esteem it a matter for sport, when we hinder others' advantages, by setting them in a wrong way. In like manner, let no one revile a person blind or dumb.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Three things in that are his, not Leviticus's. The law only forbids; Josephus adds a duty, to show the way to anyone who does not know it. The law gives no motive; Josephus names one. Sport. The pleasure of watching somebody go wrong. And he pairs the blind with the dumb, those who cannot speak, as Leviticus pairs the blind with the deaf: people who cannot catch you, and cannot answer back. So this is what a Jewish writer in the first century thought the law was guarding against. Not an accident on the road. A cruel joke, played on someone who would never know who played it.
[PAUSE 1.0]
[NARRATOR] (calm) One honest counterweight, from Henry again. He reports that the Jewish commentators before him found the literal act unthinkable.
[BOOKS] (calm) The Jewish writers, thinking it impossible that any should be so barbarous as to put a stumbling-block in the way of the blind, understood it figuratively, that it forbids giving bad counsel to those that are simple and easily imposed upon.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Both readings have been held for a very long time. A law is not proof that a thing was common. It is a sign that someone thought it worth forbidding, and here, worth a curse said aloud by a whole nation.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Brian had an earlier question too, and it is a good one.
[BRIAN] (calm) It doesn't look like Hammurabi's code has anything about the treatment of the blind. So is the Bible the first recorded anything that addresses the blind? And when was Hammurabi's code written compared with what Moses was doing in Leviticus and Deuteronomy?
[NARRATOR] (calm) Hammurabi was king of Babylon from about seventeen ninety-two to seventeen fifty before Christ. For Moses there are two main datings, one around fourteen ninety and one around twelve fifty, so Hammurabi comes first by three to five centuries. His code is the most famous law code of the ancient world, and its prologue makes a large claim. Here is the king himself, as the 1915 encyclopedia renders him.
[BOOKS] (calm) As a helper of the oppressed, as an adviser of widows and orphans, in short, as the father of his people.
[NARRATOR] (calm) But when you look for the blind in his two hundred and eighty-two laws, you find eyes only as injuries to be priced. We will hear those laws in the next episode. There is no law in Hammurabi protecting a blind person. Nor in the older code of Ur-Nammu, three centuries before him, which prices a knocked-out eye at half a mina of silver and says nothing else. So is the Bible first? Not quite. One text from Egypt does show regard, the Instruction of Amenemope, dated somewhere between thirteen hundred and ten seventy-five before Christ. In Budge's translation of 1924 it reads:
[BOOKS] (calm) Make not a laughing-stock of the blind man. Vex not the dwarf. And frustrate not the plans of the afflicted.
[NARRATOR] (calm) Notice what Amenemope forbids. Laughter. The same thing Josephus heard in the law of Moses, sport at a blind man's expense, was worth a warning on the Nile too. But it is advice from a wise man to his son. It is not law, and it carries no curse and no God who sees. As far as the shelf can tell, Leviticus nineteen fourteen is the first law in the ancient world written to protect a blind person, rather than to price his eye.
[BREAK]
[NARRATOR] (calm) Let me put the two laws together and reason it through, with no story and nobody invented. Suppose a stone is moved into a path a blind man walks every day. He falls. Who saw it? Perhaps nobody. Perhaps one man, who is not going to speak. The blind man cannot say who did it. There is no case. That is exactly why the first law says, you shall fear your God. It names the only witness there is. And suppose a traveller asks the way, and is pointed wrong, for sport, and walks an hour into the wrong valley. Who would ever prove it? Nobody. That is why the second law is not a fine. It is a curse, said by the whole nation at once, so that every man in Israel had stood on a mountainside and said Amen to it before he was ever tempted. The Law did what law can do for a wrong that leaves no witness. It made the whole people the witness, and God the judge.

It was written for the people I know who do not, and for the man who moved the stone and walked away, sure that nobody saw. Somebody saw.
