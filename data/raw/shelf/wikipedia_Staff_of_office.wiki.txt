[[File:Archdeacon by Repin.jpg|thumb|upright|Orthodox [[protodeacon]] holding a walking stick. Portrait by [[Ilya Repin]], 1877 ([[Tretyakov Gallery]], Moscow).]]
A '''staff of office''' is a staff, the carrying of which often denotes an [[official]]'s position, a [[social rank]] or a degree of social prestige.

Apart from the [[#Eccleasiastical use|ecclesiastical]] and [[#Ceremonial|ceremonial]] usages mentioned below, there are less formal usages. A [[gold]]- or [[silver]]-topped [[Walking stick|cane]] can express social standing (or [[dandyism]]). [[Teacher]]s or [[prefect]]s in schools traditionally carried less elaborate canes which marked their right (and potential threat) to administer [[caning]]s, and military officers carry a residual threat of physical punishment in their [[swagger stick]]s. [[Orchestra]]l [[Conducting|conductor]]s have in their [[Baton (conducting)|baton]]s symbols of authority as well as tools of their trade.{{citation needed|date=October 2010}}

==Ecclesiastical use==
[[File:A beadle, carrying his staff; behind him two churchwardens l Wellcome V0013366.jpg|thumb|right|upright|A [[beadle]], carrying his staff; and behind him two churchwardens with staves (coloured aquatint, 1805).]]
[[Churchwardens]] (and sometimes [[sidesman|sidesmen]]){{citation needed|date=September 2022}} traditionally carry staves or [[wand]]s on special occasions as an emblem of their office.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Meakin |first1=Tony |title=A Basic Church Dictionary |date=2001 |publisher=Canterbury Press |location=Norwich |page=81}}</ref>

In the [[Eastern Orthodox Church]] and some of the [[Oriental Orthodox Churches]] an ecclesiastical [[walking stick]] is used by [[bishop]]s,<ref name="eastern"/> [[archimandrite]]s and [[hegumen]]s (abbots) when walking outside. It is usually made of dark wood and is straight, rather than having a curved handle. The walking stick used by bishops and archimandrites is normally adorned with a silver grip at the top and a metal [[ferrule]] at the tip. The walking stick used by a hegumen or hegumenia ([[abbess]]) is normally of plain wood, unadorned.{{citation needed|date=October 2010}} The walking stick is not used in any [[liturgy|liturgical]] context, except to the extent that when a bishop is formally received at the beginning of the service, he will carry it as he walks into the church.  An [[altar server]] will usually take it from him when he enters the [[narthex]] and return it to him when he leaves at the end of the service.<ref>{{cite book |last1=Hastings |first1=James |title=Encyclopædia of Religion and Ethics: A-Art: Index |date=1921 |publisher=C. Scribner's sons |page=813 |isbn=9780567065094 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=nZ5DAQAAMAAJ&q=%22Staff+of+office%22&pg=PA813 |language=en}}</ref>

Western bishops may use a [[crosier|crozier]] or crook in a liturgical setting as a symbol of their pastoral authority.<ref name="western"/>

==State and ceremonial use==
[[File:Canada in the First World War Q30763.jpg|thumb|[[Robert Laird Borden]] inspecting the [[Usher of the Black Rod (Canada)|black rod of the Canadian Parliament]], 1918]]
Monarchs often have a rod or [[sceptre]] signifying their office, and [[field-marshal]]s are traditionally given a short thick [[Baton (military)|baton]] in several countries. There are also offices which take their titles from their staff, such as [[Black Rod]], the [[Tipstaff]], [[Gold Stick and Silver Stick]].

=== United Kingdom ===

==== White staves ====
[[File:Charles Fitzroy, 2nd Duke of Grafton by William Hoare.jpg|alt=Charles Fitzroy, 2nd Duke of Grafton by William Hoare|thumb|upright|[[Charles FitzRoy, 2nd Duke of Grafton]] (d. 1757), carrying the thin white staff of the Lord Chamberlain]]
A thin '''white staff''' or "wand" is the traditional emblem of certain [[Great Officers of State (United Kingdom)|Great Officers of State]] and high-ranking officials of the Royal Household in the United Kingdom, namely:

Great Officers of State:

* The [[Lord High Treasurer]]
* The [[Lord High Steward]]
* The [[Lord Great Chamberlain]]

Senior Officers of the Household:
* The [[Lord Steward]]
* The [[Lord Chamberlain]]
* [[Treasurer of the Household]]
* [[Comptroller of the Household]]
* [[Vice-Chamberlain of the Household]]

The "wand", which is around {{convert|5|ft|6|in}} in length, is made of white wood and has a silver plate at its base on which is engraved the name of the office to which it pertains.<ref name="Churchill1953">{{cite book |last1=Churchill |first1=Randolph Spencer |title=They Serve the Queen: A New and Authoritative Account of the Royal Household |date=1953 |publisher=Hutchinson & Co. |location=London |page=13}}</ref> The wands are carried by their holders when on duty on state or other royal occasions, such as state banquets, jubilee services and royal weddings, as well as at the [[State Opening of Parliament]] (when the Lord Great Chamberlain raises his white staff to signal to the king's messenger, Black Rod, to summon the Commons).<ref>{{cite book |last1=Liversidge |first1=Douglas |title=The House of Lords |date=1976 |publisher=Franklin Watts |location=London |page=88}}</ref>

Apart from the Lord High Steward (an office which is only now filled for coronations), all the above-listed officials were seen carrying their white staves during a [[State funerals in the United Kingdom|state funeral]].<ref name="BBCcompletefuneralguide">{{cite news |title=Your complete guide to the Queen's funeral |work=BBC News |date=19 September 2022 |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-60617519 |access-date=20 September 2022}}</ref> The Lord Chamberlain, as executive head of the Royal Household, ceremonially broke his white staff at the [[state funeral of Queen Elizabeth II]], when he automatically lost office; it was then buried with the sovereign.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bulbul |first=Nuray |date=2022-09-19 |title=What is the Wand of Office that will be broken when the Queen is laid to rest? |url=https://www.standard.co.uk/news/uk/what-is-wand-of-office-broken-queen-buried-historical-significance-b1026534.html |access-date=2022-09-20 |website=Evening Standard }}</ref> This was publicly observed at the committal service of [[Elizabeth II]] on 19 September 2022 (the first time such a service had been filmed or televised), when the incumbent, [[Andrew Parker, Baron Parker of Minsmere|Lord Parker of Minsmere]], performed this duty.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2022-09-20 |title=Explained: What is the 'Wand of Office' and why will it be broken for the Queen |url=https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/breaking-the-wand-of-office-queen-elizabeth-b2171129.html |access-date=2022-09-20 |website=The Independent }}</ref> The ritual, sometimes referred to as the "breaking of the stick",<ref>{{Cite web |date=2022-09-19 |title=What was the 'wand of office' broken at the Queen's funeral? |url=https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2022/sep/19/what-is-the-wand-of-office-that-will-be-broken-at-the-queens-funeral |access-date=2022-09-20 |website=the Guardian}}</ref> goes back many centuries (at one time all the officers of the Household who carried white staves would break them at this point and throw them into the grave, but since the 18th century the Lord Chamberlain alone has done so).<ref name="Bland1986">{{cite book |last1=Bland |first1=Olivia |title=The Royal Way of Death |date=1986 |publisher=Constable |location=London}}</ref> The staff used to be physically broken, but in modern time it is jointed in the middle and the two halves can be separated.<ref name="Churchill1953" />

The '''Lords with White Staves''' (namely the Lord Steward and Lord Chamberlain) are traditionally responsible for presenting addresses from the House of Lords to the Sovereign, and then delivering the Sovereign's response to the House.<ref name="ErskineMay1879">{{cite book |last1=Erskine May |first1=Sir Thomas |title=A Treatise on the Law, Privileges, Proceedings and Usage of Parliament |date=1879 |publisher=Butterworths |location=London |page=471 |edition=8th}}</ref> The Vice-Chamberlain of the Household today fulfils a similar role with regard to the House of Commons.<ref name="AllisonRiddell1991">{{cite book |editor1-last=Allison |editor1-first=Ronald |editor2-last=Riddell |editor2-first=Sarah |title=The Royal Encyclopedia |date=1991 |publisher=Macmillan |location=London |page=576}}</ref>

A [[queen consort]] traditionally has a separate Household, headed by her own Lord Chamberlain, who is likewise invested with a white staff.<ref>{{Cite EB1911|wstitle= Lord Chamberlain | volume= 17 | pages = 1&ndash;2 |short= 1}}</ref> Following the [[Death and funeral of Queen Elizabeth The Queen Mother|funeral]] of [[Queen Elizabeth The Queen Mother]] in 2002, her Lord Chamberlain (the [[Robert Lindsay, 29th Earl of Crawford|Earl of Crawford and Balcarres]]) attended the private interment service and likewise broke his white staff in two over her coffin.<ref>{{cite news |title=Queen says a private farewell to her mother |url=https://www.theage.com.au/world/queen-says-a-private-farewell-to-her-mother-20020411-gdu4a2.html |access-date=28 September 2022 |work=The Age |date=11 April 2002}}</ref>

==== Other state and ceremonial officers ====
[[File:Coronation of Charles III and Camilla - Coronation Procession (37).jpg|thumb|right|A Gold Staff Officer (Lt-Col. [[Nana Kofi Twumasi-Ankrah]]) marshalling the Coronation Procession in 2023.]]
The [[Earl Marshal]] and the [[Lord High Constable of England|Lord High Constable]] carry [[Baton (military)|batons]] as their insignia when on duty, reflecting the military origins of those offices.

[[Garter King-of-Arms]] has carried a gold or silver rod on state occasions since 1522. In 1906 the other [[officers of arms]] were similarly each provided with a gold-mounted ebony baton, decorated with their individual badge of office; in 1953 these were replaced with white staves, topped with a blue dove within a gold coronet.<ref name="Mansfield1980">{{cite book |last1=Mansfield |first1=Alan |title=Ceremonial Costume |date=1980 |publisher=A & C Black |location=London |pages=191–201}}</ref>

On great occasions of state the Earl Marshal may appoint officers to assist in the marshalling of processions and other duties; they are provided with a wooden staff of office as a symbol of their authority, the colour of which varies depending on the occasion: reflecting this, those on duty at Coronations are known as Gold Staff Officers, those on duty at State Funerals Purple Staff Officers, and in 1911 and 1969 those on duty at the Investiture of the Prince of Wales were termed Green Staff Officers.<ref name="AllisonRiddell1991" />

=== Zimbabwe ===
In the precolonial [[Rozvi Empire]], the {{Lang|sn|[[mambo (title)|mambo]]}} (king/emperor) gave his chiefs a staff each ({{Lang|sn|tswimbe}}) as a symbol of office.<ref name="Van Waarden-2012">{{Cite book |last=Van Waarden |first=Catrien |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=u1SqMwEACAAJ |title=Butua and the End of an Era: The Effect of the Collapse of the Kalanga State on Ordinary Citizens: an Analysis of Behaviour Under Stress |date=2012 |publisher=Archaeopress |isbn=978-1-4073-1019-0}}</ref>{{Reference page|page=63}}
