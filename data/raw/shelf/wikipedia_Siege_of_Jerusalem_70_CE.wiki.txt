The '''siege of Jerusalem''' in 70 CE was the decisive event of the [[First Jewish–Roman War|First Jewish Revolt]] against the [[Roman Empire]] (66–73 CE). Roman forces led by [[Titus]], son of the emperor [[Vespasian]], besieged [[Jerusalem]], the Jewish capital and the revolt's main stronghold. After months of fighting, they breached the defenses, destroyed the [[Second Temple]], and razed the city. Much of its population was killed, enslaved, or displaced by the Romans. The city's fall marked the effective end of the revolt and a turning point in [[Jewish history]].

In winter 69–70, following a respite in the revolt's suppression caused by a [[Year of the Four Emperors|succession war in Rome]], Titus led four [[Roman legion|legions]] and [[Auxilia|auxiliary]] forces, numbering at least 48,200 soldiers, back into [[Judaea (Roman province)|Judaea]]. By spring, this army had encircled Jerusalem, whose population had swollen with refugees and [[Passover]] pilgrims. Inside the city, rival factions led by [[John of Gischala]], [[Simon bar Giora]], and [[Eleazar ben Simon]] fought each other, destroying the city's food supplies and weakening its defenses. The factions eventually united and mounted fierce resistance, but the Romans breached the walls and drove the defenders into the Temple precincts.

In the month of [[Av (month)|Av]] (July/August), the Romans captured the [[Temple Mount]] and destroyed the Second Temple—an event commemorated annually in [[Judaism]] on [[Tisha B'Av]]. The rest of Jerusalem fell in the following weeks, and the Romans systematically demolished most of the city. A year later, Titus and Vespasian celebrated the victory with a [[Roman triumph|triumph]] in Rome, parading Temple spoils—including the [[Temple menorah|menorah]]—alongside hundreds of captives. The victory was later commemorated in monuments such as the [[Arch of Titus]] and on the {{lang|la|[[Judaea Capta coinage|Judaea capta]]}} coinage.

With [[Korban|sacrificial worship]] no longer possible, Judaism gradually reoriented around [[Torah study]], [[Chesed|acts of loving-kindness]], and [[synagogue]] prayer, a development that culminated in [[Rabbinic Judaism]]. Some [[Early Christianity|early Christians]] interpreted the destruction as a sign that [[God]] had turned away from the Jewish people, lending support to [[Supersessionism|supersessionist]] theology. After the war, [[Legio X Fretensis]] established a permanent garrison on the site. Recalling the city's restoration after its [[Siege of Jerusalem (587 BC)|destruction in 587/586 BCE]], many Jews expected the Temple [[Third Temple|to be rebuilt]], but it remained in ruins. In 130, the emperor [[Hadrian]] re-founded Jerusalem as [[Aelia Capitolina]], a [[Colonia (Roman)|Roman colony]] dedicated to [[Jupiter (god)|Jupiter]], paving the way for the last major Jewish rebellion: the [[Bar Kokhba Revolt]].

== Background ==

=== Jerusalem on the eve of the revolt ===
{{Further|Jerusalem during the Second Temple period}}[[File:20110225_Israel_0413_Jerusalem_(5539905325).jpg|thumb|upright=1.3|A scale model reconstruction of Jerusalem during the 1st century CE, part of the [[Holyland Model of Jerusalem]] at the [[Israel Museum]]|alt=Scale model of first-century Jerusalem showing the Second Temple complex at the center, the Upper and Lower City to the left, and Bezetha to the right]]

Jerusalem was a prosperous city during the first century CE, serving as the religious and political center of [[Jews|Jewish]] life both in [[Judaea (Roman province)|Judaea]] and the [[Jewish diaspora|diaspora]].{{sfn|Patrich|2024|p=3}}{{sfn|Levine|2008|pp=37–39}} The city had reached its greatest extent before the modern era, covering about {{Convert|450|acre|ha}}{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=251}}{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=35}}—nearly twice the size of the present-day [[Old City of Jerusalem|Old City]]—and was home to tens of thousands of residents.{{Efn|Historian Jonathan Price notes estimates ranging from 25,000 to over 150,000 residents.{{sfn|Price|2011|p=410}} Archaeologist Magen Broshi estimated the population at around 80,000,{{sfn|Broshi|1999|p=3}} while Orit Peleg-Barkat suggests a range of 50,000 to 80,000 people.{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=44}}}}

Jerusalem was organized into several districts.{{Efn|The ancient Jewish historian [[Josephus]] remains the single most important source for reconstructing Jerusalem's urban layout on the eve of its fall.{{sfn|Ben-Ami|Tchekhanovets|2011|p=236}}}} The Upper City, on the [[Mount Zion|southwestern hill]], was the city's wealthiest quarter and was home to the elite, including many [[Kohanim|priestly]] families. The Lower City, on the southeastern hill, was more densely populated.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=238–239}}{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=36}}{{Sfn|Grabbe|2021|pp=28–29}} The city's foremost landmark was the [[Temple Mount]], crowned by the [[Second Temple]]. The Temple had long been central to [[Jewish identity#Second Temple period|Jewish national and religious identity]],{{sfn|Patrich|2024|p=3}}{{Efn|It succeeded [[Solomon's Temple]], which was [[Siege of Jerusalem (587 BC)|destroyed by the Babylonians]] in 587/586 BCE and was rebuilt in the late 6th century BCE during the [[Return to Zion]].{{sfn|Patrich|2024|pp=4–5}}}} and under King [[Herod the Great|Herod]] (r. 37 to 4 BCE), it was expanded and rebuilt on a monumental scale, making the complex one of the largest religious precincts in the ancient world.{{sfn|Cotton|Di Segni|Eck|Isaac|2011|p=41}} The complex also hosted popular assemblies and judicial proceedings, and housed one of the city's largest marketplaces.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|pp=36–37}} Its upkeep was funded in part by an annual half-[[shekel]] tax levied on Jewish adults,{{sfn|Patrich|2024|p=15}}{{sfn|Levine|2008|p=38}} roughly equivalent to two days' wages for an ordinary laborer.{{Sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=14}}

During the [[Three Pilgrimage Festivals]],{{Efn|The holidays of [[Passover]], [[Shavuot]] (Pentecost), and [[Sukkot]] (Tabernacles), during which each [[Israelites|Israelite]] male was required by the [[Torah]] to "appear before the [[God in Judaism|Lord]]".{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|pp=498–499}}}} tens of thousands of visitors from Judaea and abroad traveled to Jerusalem to take part in Temple rituals{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=195}}{{sfn|Patrich|2024|p=15}}{{Sfn|Levine|2008|p=38}}—an influx that was a major source of the city's wealth.{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=44}} Rabbinic sources describing Jerusalem during the late Second Temple period praise its unparalleled beauty. ''[[Avot de-Rabbi Natan]]'', a late antique rabbinic work, states that "There is no beauty like the beauty of Jerusalem", while the [[Babylonian Talmud]] declares that no one who never saw Jerusalem in its splendor has seen a beautiful city.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|p=36|ps=, citing ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' (Version A), 28 and Babylonian Talmud, ''Sukkah'' 51b}} Jerusalem's reputation extended well beyond Jewish circles: the Roman author [[Pliny the Elder]], writing a few years after its destruction, retrospectively called it "by far the most famous city of the East"; the historian [[Tacitus]], in a work published three decades after the city's destruction, described it as "the capital of the Jews" and noted a temple of "enormous reaches".<ref>{{harvnb|Patrich|2024|p=3|ps=, citing [[Pliny the Elder]], ''[[Natural History (Pliny)|Natural History]]'', 5.70 and [[Tacitus]], ''[[Histories (Tacitus)|Histories]]'', 5.8}}</ref>

=== Jerusalem's defenses ===
Jerusalem was bounded by the [[Kidron Valley]] to the east and the [[Hinnom Valley]] to the south and west. These natural defenses were reinforced by successive lines of fortification. The [[Hasmonean walls|First Wall]] enclosed both the Lower and Upper City and formed the core of Jerusalem. It was constructed in the 2nd century BCE by the [[Hasmonean dynasty|Hasmonean kings]] on the foundations of an earlier wall dating to the kings of [[Kingdom of Judah|Judah]].{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=166–167}} As the city grew, the Second Wall was added further north to protect newer neighborhoods and commercial districts. Continued expansion in the early 40s CE led the Jewish client king [[Herod Agrippa]] to begin building the "Third Wall," enclosing the new northern suburb of [[Bezetha]].{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=169}}{{efn|Settled in the first century CE, its name is Aramaic for 'olive grove'.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=252}}}} The Roman emperor [[Claudius]] halted its construction, perhaps fearing rebellion or simply due to Agrippa's death. The wall was completed only hastily after the revolt had begun.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=246, 251}}{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=36}}[[File:Jerusalem_in_the_First_Century_CE.svg|thumb|upright=1.3|Map of Jerusalem in the 1st century CE|alt=Map showing the walls, neighborhoods, and fortifications of Jerusalem, along with major topographical features]]The city's defenses also included fortified structures. The [[Antonia Fortress]], positioned at the Temple Mount's northwestern corner, served as both barracks and palace, overlooking the Temple complex.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=234}}{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=35}} In the western Upper City, north of [[Herod's Palace (Jerusalem)|Herod's Palace]], stood another fortified complex (on the site of the present-day [[Tower of David]]), enclosed by walls and guarded by three towers named Phasael, Hippicus, and Mariamne.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=237–238}}{{sfn|Peleg-Barkat|2019|p=36}}

Jerusalem's large population could not be sustained by local agriculture alone, and the city therefore relied heavily on food imported from elsewhere in [[Judea]], as well as from [[Samaria]], [[Galilee]], and regions farther afield.{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=70–71}} The city also maintained food reserves.{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=105–106}}{{sfn|Rocca|2008|pp=81–82}} Its water supply relied on large [[Rainwater harvesting|rain-collecting]] pools, channels drawing from sources such as the [[Gihon Spring]], and [[Aqueduct (water supply)|aqueducts]] that brought water from the area near [[Bethlehem]] and beyond.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=254–255}} During the revolt, the influx of refugees and insurgents swelled the population further, leaving the city increasingly vulnerable to [[famine]].{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=70–71}}

=== Jerusalem during the revolt ===
{{See also|Jerusalem riots of 66|Zealot coup in Jerusalem}}
{{Further|First Jewish–Roman War}}
In the spring of 66 CE, violence broke out between Jews and Greeks in [[Caesarea Maritima]], the Roman provincial capital on the coast of Judaea.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=126–127}} [[Gessius Florus]], the [[Procurator (ancient Rome)|Roman procurator]], arrived in Jerusalem soon after and seized funds from the Temple treasury. The act triggered widespread unrest, which Florus suppressed with a massacre of civilians.{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|pp=289–290}} Florus subsequently fled the city; the client king [[Herod Agrippa II|Agrippa II]], having failed to dissuade the population from rebellion, was driven out shortly after. The Temple's [[Segan|''segan'']] (deputy), [[Eleazar ben Hanania]], then halted the daily sacrifices offered on behalf of the emperor.{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=292}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=9}}

The radical [[Sicarii]] faction, led by [[Menahem ben Judah]], raided the desert fortress of [[Masada]] and seized weapons.{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=292}} Menahem armed his followers and joined the revolt in Jerusalem, where he emerged as a leading figure.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=|pp=143, 152–153}} The insurgents drove the king's soldiers from the Upper City and burned the house of Eleazar's father, the high priest [[Ananias son of Nedebeus|Ananias]],{{Efn|The Sicarii had for years engaged in targeted violence against Jews perceived as too accommodating toward Roman rule.{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=149}}}} the royal palaces, and the public archives containing debt records.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=151}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=391}} The rebels then captured the Antonia Fortress and besieged Herod's Palace. The Jewish defenders were allowed to surrender, while the Roman soldiers withdrew to the towers of Hippicus, Phasaelus, and Mariamne. The following day, Ananias was killed by the Sicarii, along with his brother.{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=294}} Menahem appeared in public in royal attire before being killed along with many of his followers by Eleazar's faction; a few survivors escaped to Masada, including Menahem's relative Eleazar ben Yair, who later became its leader.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=|pp=153–154}} In mid-September, the remaining Roman soldiers, negotiating with Eleazar ben Hanania, surrendered after being promised safe passage out of the city, but were nevertheless massacred by his men.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=143}}{{Efn|The sole survivor was the Roman commander Metilius, who was spared after pledging to [[conversion to Judaism|convert to Judaism]] and undergo [[brit mila|circumcision]].{{sfn|Rogers|2021|p=143}}}}

With Jerusalem in open revolt, [[Gaius Cestius Gallus]], the governor of [[Roman Syria]], marched into Judaea in the fall of 66 to suppress the rebellion. He advanced on Jerusalem with an army of approximately 30,000 soldiers, reaching [[Mount Scopus]] and burning the northern suburb of Bezetha.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=204}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=174}} He then retreated unexpectedly{{Efn|Josephus suggested that Gallus could have captured the city with more determination.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=71}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=175,177}} Modern historians have proposed various explanations. [[Menahem Stern]] suggested that Gallus, facing strong resistance, doubted he could seize the city;{{sfn|Stern|1976|p=300}} [[E. Mary Smallwood]] proposed that he may have been concerned about the approaching winter, a lack of siege equipment, the risk of ambushes in the hills, and the potential insincerity of the moderates' offer to open the gates.{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=295}}}} and in [[Cheshvan]] (October/November) 66 was [[Battle of Beth Horon (66)|ambushed by Jewish forces]] at [[Bethoron]], losing nearly an entire legion.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=71}}{{sfn|Mason|2016|p=281}} Following Gallus's defeat, a [[Judean provisional government|provisional Jewish government]] was established in Jerusalem, appointing commanders across the country and completing the city's Third Wall.{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=51, 70}} [[John of Gischala]] led an independent rebel faction in Galilee, while [[Simon bar Giora]] led another in Judea.{{sfn|Gabba|1999|pp=160, 290}}

In 67, the Roman emperor [[Nero]] appointed the general [[Vespasian]] to suppress the revolt.{{sfn|Millar|1995|pp=71–72}}{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=306}} Vespasian conducted a [[Galilee campaign (67)|methodical campaign across Galilee]], subduing rebel strongholds one by one. Meanwhile, Jerusalem grew increasingly unstable as refugees and rebel factions, including John of Gischala and his followers,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=265}} poured into the city.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=86}} The [[Zealots]], a priestly anti-Roman faction led by [[Eleazar ben Simon]], and the Idumaeans{{Efn|A group residing south of Judaea, the Idumaeans were converted to Judaism by the Hasmonean leader [[John Hyrcanus]] after their conquest in the second century BCE.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=273}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=89}}}} [[Zealot coup in Jerusalem|overthrew]] the city's moderate leadership.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=267–278}}{{Efn|The Zealots, encouraged by John of Gischala, invited the Idumaeans into Jerusalem under the pretext that the moderate leadership was betraying the city to Rome. The Idumaeans entered during a storm and, together with the Zealots, massacred soldiers, priests, and civilians.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=273–277}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=89–90}} Many Idumaeans later withdrew in regret; others went on to join Simon bar Giora.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=279–280}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=91, 93}}}} By 68, Vespasian had subdued most of Judaea, but Nero's suicide plunged Rome into the succession conflict known as the [[Year of the Four Emperors]].{{sfn|Price|1992|p=102}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=289–290}} Vespasian postponed the siege of Jerusalem; he reportedly preferred to let the rival Jewish factions exhaust one another while he waited for the spring harvest.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=281–282}} In 69, Vespasian was proclaimed ''[[imperator]]'' by his troops and departed for Italy to secure the imperial throne,{{sfn|Gabba|1999|p=163}} entrusting command of the campaign to his son, [[Titus]].{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=129}}

In Jerusalem itself, civil war broke out among the rebel factions.{{sfn|Rocca|2008|pp=81–82}} By spring 69, Simon bar Giora's forces were camped outside the city, attacking deserters, while radical factions inside terrorized the population.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=294}} Hoping to weaken John of Gischala, his opponents admitted Simon to the city.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=294}} However, Simon's arrival deepened the conflict instead. [[Josephus]], a captured Jewish commander who observed the siege from the Roman side and later [[The Jewish War|chronicled the war]] under Roman patronage, describes Jerusalem as descending into a three-way civil war, with each faction fighting the other two; Tacitus similarly notes that the city was divided among three generals and three armies.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=283}} John of Gischala, Eleazar ben Simon, and Simon bar Giora divided the city between them: John held most of the Temple Mount and the southeastern hill, Eleazar the inner Temple court, and Simon, with the largest force, the remainder.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=205–206}} In the course of the fighting, the warring factions burned the city's food stores, destroying provisions that would have been critical once the siege began.{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=105–106}}{{sfn|Rocca|2008|pp=81–82}}

=== Reported omens before the destruction ===
Josephus recounts several omens said to have foretold the destruction of Jerusalem in the years before it fell.{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=420|ps=, citing Josephus, ''The Jewish War'', 6.288–299}} Among those were "a star resembling a sword, which stood over the city, and a [[comet]] that continued a whole year"—possibly [[Halley's Comet]], which was visible over Jerusalem in the winter and spring of 66.{{Sfn|Horowitz|1996|pp=458–459}}{{Sfn|Vette|2023|pp=658–659}} Other reported signs included a sudden bright light around the Temple altar near the festival of [[Passover]], a cow giving birth to a lamb, and the massive eastern gate of the Temple's inner sanctuary swinging open on its own.{{Sfn|Vette|2023|pp=658–659}} Josephus also reports armies in the sky before sunset, and recounts that during the pilgrimage festival of [[Shavuot]], priests in the Temple heard a loud noise followed by a voice declaring, "We are departing from here."{{Sfn|Vette|2023|pp=658–659}} Beginning four years before the revolt, a peasant named [[Jesus ben Ananias]] wandered the city prophesying its destruction, continuing to do so for more than seven years until he was killed by a Roman projectile during the siege.{{Sfn|Millar|1999|p=166}}

== Titus's march on Jerusalem and early engagements ==
In winter 69/70, Titus marched from [[Roman Egypt|Egypt]] through the coastal plain to Caesarea Maritima, where he established his main base.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=73}} Four [[Roman legion|legions]]—[[Legio V Macedonica|V Macedonica]], [[Legio X Fretensis|X Fretensis]], [[Legio XII Fulminata|XII Fulminata]], and [[Legio XV Apollinaris|XV Apollinaris]]—formed the core of his army, supported by detachments of the [[Legio III Cyrenaica]] and the [[Legio XXII Deiotariana]] from Egypt, twenty infantry [[Auxilia#cohortes|cohorts]], eight cavalry {{lang|la|[[Auxilia#alae|alae]]}}, Syrian irregulars, and auxiliaries supplied by allied [[Client kingdoms in ancient Rome|client kings]].{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=75}} Tacitus also records the participation of "a strong force of Arabs", whom he described as driven by longstanding hostility toward Jews.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=75}} In total, this combined force numbered at least 48,200 soldiers,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=302}} making it considerably larger than the force deployed for the [[Roman conquest of Britain|invasion of Britain]] in 43 CE.{{sfn|Millar|2005|p=101}} Titus's second-in-command was [[Tiberius Julius Alexander]], a Jewish-born [[Equestrian (Roman)|equestrian]] governor and general who had reportedly renounced his ancestral traditions.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=147}}[[File:Bust_of_Titus_(loan_from_Schloss_Erbach)_-_Glyptothek_-_Munich_-_Germany_2017.jpg|thumb|upright=1.05|Marble bust of [[Titus]], son of emperor [[Vespasian]], who returned to [[Judaea (Roman province)|Judaea]] in the winter of 69/70 at the head of an army of some 48,200|alt=A marble bust of the Roman Emperor Titus wearing detailed armor, showcased on a pedestal inside a museum gallery]]Meanwhile, infighting continued within Jerusalem.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=300}} Eleazar ben Simon had fortified his position in the Temple's inner court, seizing its stores of [[Bikkurim (periodical)|consecrated first fruits]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=300}} John of Gischala attacked him from below the Temple's sacred gates, while Simon bar Giora, who held the Upper and Lower cities, struck at John in turn. The factions employed artillery in the fighting, killing many, including among priests and worshippers.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=300–301}}

In early [[Nisan]] 70 (March/April), Titus set out from Caesarea Maritima with Legio XII Fulminata and Legio XV Apollinaris, marching toward Jerusalem.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=127}} The Roman army advanced through Samaria, halting to rest at [[Jifna|Gophna]], about {{Convert|13|mi|km}} north of Jerusalem.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=303}} Meanwhile, Legio V Macedonica, under [[Sextus Vettulenus Cerialis]], approached from the west via [[Emmaus]], while Legio X Fretensis, commanded by Aulus Larcius Lepidus Sulpicianus, advanced from the east through [[Jericho]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=303}} Titus's own force then made camp in the "Valley of Thorns" near [[Gibeah]], about {{Convert|3|mi|km}} from Jerusalem.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=304}} Like earlier commanders who had attacked the city—[[Assyrian siege of Jerusalem|Sennacherib]], [[Siege of Jerusalem (587 BC)|Nebuchadnezzar II]], [[Siege of Jerusalem (63 BC)|Pompey]], and Herod—Titus directed his assault against Jerusalem's northern and northeastern flanks, the only sides not protected by ravines.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=305}}{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=319}}

Tacitus put the number of people besieged in Jerusalem at 600,000, including "every age and both sexes". He states that "all who were able bore arms", and regarded death as preferable to exile from their home.{{sfn|Clarysse|2021|p=31|ps=, citing Tacitus, ''Histories'', 5.13.3}} Josephus gives a far higher figure of 1.1 million dead over the course of the siege, among them Passover pilgrims trapped in the city when the siege closed.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=369}}<ref>{{harvnb|Price|1992|p=156|ps=, citing ''War'', 6.420–421}}</ref> While rejecting these figures as exaggerated, historian [[Menahem Stern]] writes that Josephus's mention of 23,400 armed men in Jerusalem on the eve of the siege is realistic.{{sfn|Stern|1980|pp=62–63}} The city was also crowded with refugees from Judea, Galilee, and [[Idumaea]].{{sfn|Price|2024|p=18}} The rival factions ended their internal conflict and united when the Romans began attacking Jerusalem's walls.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=309–310}}

Titus led a reconnaissance force of about 600 cavalry to assess Jerusalem's northern defenses, narrowly escaping an ambush after being cut off from the main body of his troops.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=305}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=128}} He then advanced to Mount Scopus, northeast of the city, where he established camps for Legio XII, XV, and V.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=306}} Legio X Fretensis camped separately on the [[Mount of Olives]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=306}} Its soldiers were still building their fortifications, many of them unarmed, when a combined force from the rival factions crossed the Kidron Valley and surprised them.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=307}}{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=318}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=128–130}} The assault was repelled only after Titus intervened in person.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=307}} John of Gischala and Simon bar Giora reconciled,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=307}} though their factions continued to operate under separate command,{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=115, 128}} maintaining their division of the city.{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=115, 128}}{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=319}}

== Battle for Jerusalem's walls ==
On 14 Nisan, the start of the Passover festival, the Romans took advantage of a temporary halt in Jewish attacks to move their siege forces closer to Jerusalem's walls.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=129}} That same night, John of Gischala's men took advantage of the opening of the Temple's inner gates for festival worshippers: entering among the crowd with weapons concealed, they overpowered the Zealots stationed in the courts and seized the sanctuary.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=309–310}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=129}}{{sfn|Smallwood|1976|p=318}} Some of the defeated Zealots fled to underground hiding places beneath the Temple Mount,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=100}} while others joined John's faction; Eleazar ben Simon retained a command role under John's leadership.{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=413}} Roman historian [[Cassius Dio]], writing in Greek more than a century later, stated that Titus had offered the Jews terms of surrender before the siege began, but they rejected his offer.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=304–305|ps=, citing [[Cassius Dio]], ''Roman History'', 65.4.1}}

The Romans opened their assault against the Third Wall.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=129}} Internal fighting briefly resumed, with Simon bar Giora's forces engaged on two fronts, against John inside the Temple complex and against the Romans as their siegeworks advanced,{{sfn|Price|1992|p=130}} though the two factions appear to have reached a truce shortly afterward.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=131}} Once the Romans completed their siegeworks, Jewish forces launched an offensive that initially gained ground before being repelled by Roman cavalry. During or shortly after this engagement, the Idumaean commander John ben Sosas was killed by an arrow; Titus had a Jewish captive [[Crucifixion|crucified]] within sight of the walls to intimidate the defenders, a tactic he would use repeatedly during the siege.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=131}}

Fifteen days into the siege works, a Roman battering ram breached the Third Wall after repeated Jewish attempts to set fire to the engines. Within five days, the Romans broke through the middle section of the Second Wall.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=134}} The breach was too narrow, and Roman troops pushing through it were caught in the lanes beyond,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=318–319}} where defenders who knew the ground inflicted heavy losses and drove them back.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=319}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=134}} Four days later, the Romans returned, widened the breach, and captured the area.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=320}} They then levelled the northern quarter and halted operations for several days, during which desertions to the Roman lines increased.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=135}}

== From Antonia to the Temple Mount ==
When hostilities resumed, Titus constructed [[Battering ram|siege ramps]] at the Antonia Fortress and against the towers of the Upper City, while also employing [[psychological warfare]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=320–321}} He staged a four-day parade of cavalry and infantry in polished armor and distributed their pay in view of the walls.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=320}} He again offered peace terms through Josephus, who addressed the population in their "ancestral tongue"—likely [[Hebrew language|Hebrew]], though possibly [[Aramaic]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=320}} Josephus argued that the Romans had respected Jewish holy sites, unlike the rebels who had endangered them, and urged repentance, claiming that Rome's victories showed that [[God in Judaism|God]] now favored Roman rule.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=323}}{{sfn|Wilker|2012|pp=181–182}} Josephus says he was struck by a stone from the wall; he responded by recounting [[Jewish history]] from the [[The Exodus|Exodus]] to the [[Return to Zion|return from Babylonian exile]], arguing that past victories had come through obedience to divine will rather than armed resistance. Comparing himself to the prophet [[Jeremiah]], he warned that the true struggle was not against Rome but against God, and again urged the defenders to surrender in order to spare the city, the Temple, and their families.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=324–325}}{{sfn|Wilker|2012|pp=181–182}} The rebels refused—whether out of trust in divine protection, confidence in their fortifications, or fear of the humiliation and torture that awaited them if captured.{{Sfn|Kelley|2022|p=15}}

Within the city, the rebel factions attacked those trying to flee and ransacked wealthy homes for food, torturing householders suspected of hoarding.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=325–326}} Outside the walls, Roman soldiers tortured and crucified captured Jews in sight of the defenders, nailing them to crosses in different positions as a form of mockery. Josephus states that about 500 were captured each day, sometimes more, and that Titus did not prevent the executions, hoping the spectacle would encourage surrender.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=327}}{{Sfn|Kelley|2022|p=15}} Syrian and Arab auxiliaries reportedly [[Disembowelment|disemboweled]] refugees in search of swallowed valuables.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=333–334}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=161}}[[File:19_Shrine_of_the_Book_005.jpg|thumb|upright=1.35|Scale model reconstruction of the [[Second Temple]] complex and the [[Antonia Fortress]] (upper right), part of the [[Holyland Model of Jerusalem]] at the [[Israel Museum]]|alt=Scale model of the Second Temple in first-century Jerusalem, showing the sanctuary at the center, the surrounding Temple courts enclosed by colonnades and walls, the Royal Stoa along the southern edge, and the city beyond]]As grain prices soared, residents scavenged for scraps in the sewers. Meanwhile, large numbers of corpses accumulated outside the city. Many starved; others died of diseases brought on by hunger. Josephus describes children with [[Kwashiorkor|swollen bellies]] and deserters showing signs of what appears to have been [[dropsy]]. The later exegesis ''[[Lamentations Rabbah]]'' preserves a tradition in which rabbi [[Eleazar bar Zadok]] recalls that his father's body never fully recovered from the famine, even decades later; the same source describes a woman whose [[Hair loss|hair fell out]] from [[malnutrition]].{{sfn|Price|1992|p=156|ps=, citing ''War'', 5.513 and ''[[Lamentations Rabbah]]'', 4.11}}

The legions began raising four ramps against the Antonia Fortress on 12 [[Iyar]] (April/May) and completed them seventeen days later. John of Gischala countered by tunneling beneath the works, shoring the mine with timber and setting it alight; the props burned through and the ramps collapsed into the cavity.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=329}} Two days later, Simon bar Giora's men destroyed the engines in the western sector.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=329}} The Romans rebuilt their machines and, according to Josephus, completed a [[Investment (military)|circumvallation]] wall some 39 [[Stadion (unit)|stadia]] (4.5 miles or 7.2 kilometers) long, with thirteen forts, in just three days, cutting off supply lines and escape routes.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=329}} Titus then ordered fresh ramps raised against Antonia. The Romans stormed the outworks on 1 [[Tammuz (Hebrew month)|Tammuz]] (June/July), and that night the wall gave way at the very point John had mined; the fortress fell shortly afterwards.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=332, 340–341}}

Inside the city, Simon bar Giora executed members of the elite and suspected advocates of surrender, throwing their mutilated bodies beyond the walls.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=332–333}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=160–161}} Some residents tried to escape by jumping from the walls; others went out as if to skirmish and then deserted to the Romans.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=333}} John and his followers reportedly plundered the Temple, melting down sacred vessels, consuming consecrated food, and distributing sacred oil and wine among their supporters.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=334–335}} The famine grew more severe, claiming many lives. Josephus recounts the story of a woman named Maria from [[Perea]], a region in [[Transjordan (region)|Transjordan]]. After rebels plundered her possessions, she [[Human cannibalism|killed, roasted, and ate]] her infant son. When rebel fighters later came searching for food, drawn by the smell of cooking, she offered them what remained of the child; horrified, they fled the house.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=349–350}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=155}}

The fighting carried on into the Temple complex, where the Romans gained ground before being pushed back after some ten hours.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=340}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=164}} A [[centurion]] named Iulianus temporarily drove the Jewish defenders back into the complex single-handedly before slipping, being surrounded, and killed.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=340–341}} Titus ordered the foundations of the Antonia to be dug out to open a route for his army.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=341|ps=. According to Rogers, the ''tamid'' ceased "either because of a lack of men, as the mistakenly copied text reads or, more likely, of (two) lambs, as edited."}} According to Josephus, on [[Seventeenth of Tammuz|17 Tammuz]], the [[Burnt offering (Judaism)|daily temple sacrifice]] ({{tlit|he|Tamid}}) ceased for lack of priests or lambs.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=341|ps=. According to Rogers, the ''tamid'' ceased "either because of a lack of men, as the mistakenly copied text reads or, more likely, of (two) lambs, as edited."}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=165–166}}{{Efn|The ''Mishnah'', ''Ta'anit'' 4:6 independently lists the cessation of the ''tamid'' on 17 Tammuz among the calamities of that date.}} Titus again offered peace through Josephus, who addressed the defenders "in the language of the Hebrews", but they refused.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=341–342}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=415}} Josephus also conveyed Titus's offer to let John resume the sacrifices with any Jews he wished to appoint, but John refused, declaring that he had no fear of the city's capture because it belonged to God.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=341–342}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=415}} Whether or not the exchange occurred as described, it reflects a belief, dating back to the Kingdom of Judah, that [[Zion]] enjoyed divine protection.{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=415}}

Some priests and members of the nobility surrendered and were sent by Titus to Gophna. Titus later recalled them and had them walk around the city walls with Josephus to encourage further defections; many more residents then fled to the Romans.{{sfn|Van Kooten|2011|p=442|ps=, citing ''War'', 6.113–119}} The Romans then built four ramps aimed at the Temple's defenses.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=345}} The defenders set fire to the northwest [[stoa]] (portico) connecting the Temple to the Antonia Fortress to block Roman access, while the Romans burned the adjoining portico two days later, on 24 Tammuz.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=346–349}} After repeated failed attempts to breach the Temple walls with battering rams, the Romans set fire to its gates and porticoes, forcing the defenders back into the inner court.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=351–352}} Josephus reports that Titus then convened his commanders to debate the Temple's fate; and that Titus personally opposed those urging its destruction, arguing that so magnificent a structure should be preserved as an ornament of Roman rule.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=352–353|ps=, citing ''War'', 6.237–243}}

== Destruction of the Second Temple ==
According to Josephus, on 8 [[Av (month)|Av]] (July/August), two legions completed their ramps against the Temple. When the battering rams failed to breach the masonry, Titus ordered the gates set alight; the fire spread through the porticoes, and the outer court fell into Roman hands.{{sfn|Bahat|1999|p=42}} Two days later, on 10 Av, a Roman soldier hurled a burning piece of wood through a window on the Temple's north side, igniting a fire that ultimately consumed the structure.{{sfn|Bahat|1999|p=42}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=355}}{{Efn|Josephus dates the burning to the tenth of Av and observes that the First Temple had been destroyed on the same day (''War'', 6.250). Rabbinic tradition instead places both destructions on the ninth (Mishnah, ''Ta'anit'' 4:6), the date observed as Tisha B'Av. The Talmud (''Ta'anit'' 29a) already registers the difficulty, noting that [[Books of Kings|2 Kings]] 25:8 gives the seventh of the month and [[Book of Jeremiah|Jeremiah]] 52:12 the tenth, and reconciles them by holding that the fire was set late on the ninth and consumed the building on the tenth.}} Josephus writes that Titus, roused from rest, rushed to the scene and ordered the fire extinguished, but that some soldiers either failed to hear or disregarded his orders, with some urging others to spread the flames.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=355}} Titus and his commanders then entered the Temple, where he again ordered the soldiers to extinguish the fire, but many continued their actions, motivated by their [[Antisemitism#Ancient world|hatred of Jews]] and hope of plunder.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=357}}

As the Temple burned, Roman soldiers killed everyone they encountered, whether they resisted or pleaded for their lives. Soldiers climbed over piles of corpses as they pursued those attempting to flee.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=360}}{{Efn|Josephus writes that the flames, battle cries, and screams combined into a deafening sound that could be heard as far as Perea.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=360}}}} The priests reportedly continued performing their ritual duties until they were killed, with many accepting death willingly. Josephus recounts that two priests, Meirus son of Belgas and Joseph son of Daleus, threw themselves into the flames and died alongside the Temple. Cassius Dio writes that "the Jews defended themselves much more vigorously than before", treating the chance to fight and die beside the temple as a stroke of "rare good fortune"; they soon "met death willingly, some throwing themselves on the swords of the Romans, some slaying one another, others taking their own lives, and still others leaping into the flames".{{sfn|Levine|1984|p=297|ps=, citing ''War'', 6.280 and Cassius Dio, ''Roman History'', 65.6.2–3}} ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' similarly relates that young priests, seeing the sanctuary ablaze, cast the Temple keys toward heaven, declaring themselves unworthy custodians before leaping together into the fire.{{sfn|Goodblatt|2006|pp=184–185|ps=, citing ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' (Version B), 7}}

Roman soldiers looted and killed indiscriminately, sparing no one, including those who begged for their lives.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=360}} According to Josephus, some 6,000 people, many of them poor women and children, had taken refuge in a colonnade in the outer court; the Romans set it ablaze, killing everyone inside.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=361}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=171–172}} Josephus blames the deaths on "false prophets" who had urged the crowd to gather on the Temple Mount, promising that doing so would bring deliverance.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=361}}

The Romans then systematically destroyed what remained of the Temple Mount, razing its porticoes, treasuries, and gates.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=171}}{{sfn|Bahat|1999|pp=42–43}} Soldiers carried their military standards into the Temple court and offered sacrifices before them, then hailed Titus as imperator.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=362}}{{sfn|Bahat|1999|pp=42–43}} The soldiers took so much plunder that, according to Josephus, that the price of gold in Syria fell by half.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=362}} A group of priests attempted to surrender, but Titus had them executed, declaring it fitting that they die alongside the Temple.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=362}} With the destruction of the Temple complex complete, Roman forces turned to demolishing the rest of the city.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=368}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=172}}

=== Responsibility for the destruction ===

Whether the destruction was intentional, and how much responsibility rests with Titus personally, remains a matter of scholarly dispute.{{sfn|Goodman|2004|p=16}}{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|pp=194–195}}{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}} [[Sulpicius Severus]], a Christian historian writing in 4th-century [[Gaul]], offers a different account. Possibly drawing on Tacitus's lost narrative in the ''Histories,{{Efn|1=First proposed by Jacob Bernays in the mid-nineteenth century,{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=208}} this theory remains disputed.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=354}} Another interpretation suggests that Sulpicius's account was derived from Josephus but was reworked for theological purposes, portraying the temple's destruction as divine punishment of the Jewish people.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=208}}}}'' he states that Titus deliberately ordered the Temple's destruction in order to abolish Judaism and Christianity. The Babylonian Talmud likewise portrays Titus as directly responsible for the destruction.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=354}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|pp=417, 419|ps=, citing [[Sulpicius Severus]], ''Chronica'', 2.30, 6–7, and Babylonian Talmud, ''[[Gittin]]'', 56b}}[[File:Nicolas_Poussin_-_The_Destruction_and_Sack_of_the_Temple_of_Jerusalem_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg|thumb|''[[The Capture of Jerusalem by Titus (Poussin)|The Destruction and Sack of the Temple of Jerusalem]]'', 17th-century painting by [[Nicolas Poussin]]|alt=17th-century painting of Roman soldiers storming and setting fire to the Second Temple in Jerusalem. Armed troops battle amid flames and chaos as civilians flee around a colonnaded entrance. Following Josephus's account in The Jewish War, Titus is depicted urging his soldiers to stop the assault, but to no avail]]Given Jerusalem's dry summer heat, historian [[Martin Goodman (historian)|Martin Goodman]] considers Josephus's account—that a single burning piece of wood rapidly set the building alight—to be plausible.{{sfn|Goodman|2004|p=16}} Most modern scholars, however, reject Josephus's version.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|pp=194–195}} [[Lester L. Grabbe]] calls it "unbelievable", arguing that a Roman commander known for harsh discipline, including the use of the death penalty, would not have tolerated his soldiers openly disobeying orders in his presence.{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=418}} Since the destruction of temples was regarded in antiquity as an act of sacrilege,{{efn|According to [[Benjamin Isaac]], the destruction of sanctuaries has been a common wartime practice dating back to at least the fifth century BCE.{{sfn|Isaac|2024|p=235}}}} some scholars suggest that Josephus may have downplayed Titus's role in order to protect Titus's reputation.{{sfn|Goodman|2004|p=16}} Grabbe and Fausto Parente argue instead that Josephus's version was meant to discourage Jews from seeking future vengeance for the Temple's destruction.{{sfn|Parente|2005|pp=67, 70}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|pp=417–418}}

Several modern historians view the destruction as deliberate and ideologically motivated. [[Doron Mendels]] argues that the Romans targeted the Temple because they regarded it, and Jerusalem more broadly, as the center of Jewish rebellion.{{sfn|Mendels|1992|p=368}} James Rives maintains that the goal was "not only to forestall future revolts but also to eliminate the anomalous cultic organization that hindered the integration of Jews into the empire".{{sfn|Rives|2005|p=165}} [[Steve Mason (biblical scholar)|Steve Mason]] argues that Titus and the Flavian regime treated the Temple's destruction not as an embarrassment but as a military necessity, noting that Rome had previously destroyed temples at [[Siege of Carthage (Third Punic War)|Carthage]] and [[Battle of Corinth (146 BC)|Corinth]], and that nothing in the historical record suggests Titus regarded the act as shameful. He also points to the ''Argonautica'' of the Roman poet [[Valerius Flaccus (poet)|Valerius Flaccus]], which celebrates Titus as "begrimed with the dust of Jerusalem", hurling firebrands and raging on every turret, as evidence that the destruction was not presented as a source of shame.{{Sfn|Mason|2011|pp=233–238}}

According to Doron Lopez, the Temple's destruction was a characteristic, perhaps even inadvertent, consequence of capturing an enemy city, common in Roman conquests generally. He notes that despite Rome's pious self-image, dozens of cases show piety being set aside amid the thirst for plunder or destruction. Lopez rejects the idea that the destruction targeted Judaism specifically, pointing to its absence from Flavian propaganda, and argues this interpretation instead reflects a Jewish perspective on the event.{{Sfn|Lopez|2021|pp=87–90}}

== Conquest of the Lower and Upper City ==
{{Further|Burnt House|Second Temple#Archaeology}}
After the Temple's destruction, some rebels sought a [[parley]] with Titus, meeting on a bridge overlooking the ruins, possibly at what is now [[Wilson's Arch (Jerusalem)|Wilson's Arch]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=363}} Hoping to save what remained of the city, they were met instead with a rebuke: Titus, speaking through an interpreter (possibly Josephus), condemned them for their repeated ingratitude and rejection of peace terms dating back to Pompey's time.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=363}} He offered them one final chance to surrender, but the rebels demanded safe passage to the desert for themselves and their families.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=364}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} Offended by the demand, Titus broke off negotiations and declared that he would offer no further terms.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=364}}

The following day, Titus authorized the burning and sacking of what remained of the city.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=364}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} Roman soldiers set fire to the city archives, the [[Acra (fortress)|Acra fortress]], the council chamber, and the Ophel district,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=364}} and the blaze spread through the Lower City, eventually reaching [[Helena of Adiabene]]'s palace and the [[Pool of Siloam]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=364–365}}{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=172–173}} Titus granted protection to the family of King [[Izates II|Izates]] of [[Adiabene]], who were subsequently taken to Rome as hostages.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=364}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} The Romans found little plunder in the captured areas, as the rebels had already stripped them of anything valuable.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=365}} The rebels then retreated to Herod's Palace, massacred those inside, and looted its contents.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=365}} They captured two Roman soldiers and killed one; the second escaped before he could be executed, and Titus [[Honesta missio|discharged him in disgrace]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=365}} The rebels mocked Josephus's renewed appeals and withdrew into the tunnels beneath the city, hoarding food and killing anyone who came upon them.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=365}}

Jerusalem's Upper City was the last district to fall.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}} On 20 Av, the Romans launched a fresh assault,{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=366}} constructing siege ramps to the northwest and northeast.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}} Five Idumaean leaders secretly approached Titus to negotiate surrender, and he agreed, hoping their capitulation would encourage others to follow.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=366}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} Simon bar Giora, however, discovered the plot, executed the five envoys, and imprisoned the remaining leaders.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=366}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} Despite this, many Idumaeans still defected to the Romans; those who held Roman citizenship were released, while the rest were sold into slavery.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=366}} A priest named Jesus son of Thebuthi surrendered a number of sacred items from the sanctuary, including two lampstands, golden tables, garments, and precious stones, while another priest, Phineas, secured a pardon by handing over priestly vestments and the spices used in ritual offerings.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=366–367}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=174}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} These objects were later paraded in Rome during Titus's [[Roman triumph|triumph]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=381–383}}{{sfn|Hengel|1989|p=375}}

Within eighteen days, the Romans completed their siege works and, on 7 [[Elul]] (August/September), launched their final assault.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=174}} Once the Upper City's wall was breached, resistance collapsed quickly.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}} The defenders abandoned the towers of Herod's citadel, which Josephus considered impregnable to anything but starvation.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=174}} Their last counterattack repelled, the rebel leaders fled into the city's underground tunnels.{{sfn|Price|1992|p=174}} Roman forces raised their standards over the captured towers and carried out a massacre, cutting down civilians in the streets and in their homes;{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}} some families were found already dead of starvation, while others were killed outright.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}} The killing ceased only with nightfall. By morning, Jerusalem was entirely engulfed in flames.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=367}}

== Destruction of Jerusalem ==
{{Further|Archaeological remnants of the Jerusalem Temple|Jerusalem Archaeological Park|Herodian Quarter|Burnt House|Excavations at the Temple Mount}}

By early September, the conquest of Jerusalem was complete.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}} According to Josephus, Titus ordered the city razed,{{sfn|Price|2011|p=409}}{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}} except for a few structures:{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=238, 294}}
{{Blockquote|text=The army now having no victims either for Jerusalem slaughter or plunder, through lack of all objects on which to vent their rage—for they would assuredly never have desisted through a desire to spare anything so long as there was work to be done—Caesar ordered the whole city and the temple to be razed to the ground, leaving only the loftiest of the towers, Phasael, Hippicus, and Mariamme,{{Efn|[[Tower of David (northeast tower)|One]] of these towers still stands within the [[Tower of David]] complex. Most scholars identify it as Phasael, though Hillel Geva has argued that it should instead be identified as Hippicus.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=238}}}} and the portion of the wall enclosing the city on the west: the latter as an encampment for the garrison that was to remain, and the towers to indicate to posterity the nature of the city and of the strong defences which had yet yielded to Roman prowess. All the rest of the wall encompassing the city was so completely levelled to the ground as to leave future visitors to the spot no ground for believing that it had ever been inhabited. Such was the end to which the frenzy of revolutionaries brought Jerusalem, that splendid city of world-wide renown.|author=Josephus|title=''The Jewish War'''|source=VII, 1.1 (Translated by [[Henry St. John Thackeray]])}}
Archaeological remains of destruction have been found across the ancient city, corroborating Josephus's account.<ref>{{harvnb|Weksler-Bdolah|2019|p=4}}: "The historical description is consistent with the archaeological finds."</ref><ref>{{harvnb|Price|2011|pp=409–410}}: "The destruction layer of 70 C.E., found by archaeologists throughout the city, revealing massive razing and burning of structures, fits Josephus' description."</ref> Excavations along the southwestern foot of the Temple Mount—directed by [[Benjamin Mazar]] and Meir Ben-Dov in 1968–1978 and renewed by [[Ronny Reich]] and Ya'akov Billig in 1994–1998{{Sfn|Reich|2015|p=398}}—uncovered massive piles of stones from the [[Western Wall]], fallen onto the paved street below.{{Sfn|Reich|Billig|2008|pp=1809–1811}}{{Sfn|Magness|2024|pp=253, 260}} The wall's upper courses appear to have been dismantled course by course.{{Sfn|Reich|Baruch|2020|pp=104–105}}{{Efn|Shimon Gibson has proposed that the massive stone collapse resulted from the [[363 Galilee earthquake|earthquake of 363 CE]]. Reich and Baruch reject this interpretation, citing, among other evidence, the absence of coins dated later than 69 CE beneath the collapse, indications that the wall's upper courses were systematically dismantled rather than brought down in a single seismic event, and the reuse of stones as early as 79 CE.{{sfn|Reich|Baruch|2020|pp=99–105}}}} Among the rubble are stones from [[Robinson's Arch]], a monumental staircase leading up to the Temple precinct, including remains of its parapets.{{Sfn|Reich|2015|p=398}}{{Sfn|Reich|Baruch|2020|p=105}} One of the arch's fallen parapet stones was later reused as a [[Miliarium|Roman milestone]] bearing an inscription naming Vespasian and Titus; because Titus is styled ''imperator'', Reich dates the inscription to his reign (79–81), indicating that the arch had already been destroyed by then.{{Sfn|Reich|2015|p=405}} Also found among the rubble were broken [[Pilaster|pilasters]], some bearing signs of burning.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=219}} Near the southern end of the [[Western Wall]], excavators recovered the [[Trumpeting Place inscription]], a stone marking the spot where a priest would sound a trumpet to announce the beginning and end of [[Shabbat]].{{Sfn|Demsky|1986|pp=50–52}}{{Efn|The inscription's placement corresponds to a station described by Josephus, who notes that Jewish rebels built a tower above this same spot to gain height for hurling missiles at the Roman legionaries below.{{sfn|Demsky|1986|pp=50–52}}}}[[File:NinthAvStonesWesternWall.JPG|thumb|Stones from the Temple Mount complex lying on the street below at the Western Wall |alt=Ancient Herodian carved stone blocks scattered in a debris pile at the foot of Jerusalem's Western Wall]]

Mazar's excavations also recovered hundreds of architectural fragments below the southern wall, later studied by Orit Peleg-Barkat. In consultation with geologist Aryeh Shimron, she attributed many of them on stylistic grounds to the [[Royal Stoa (Jerusalem)|Royal Stoa]], a [[basilica]] that stood at the complex's southern end. Shimron's analysis found white crusts and gray discoloration on the stones consistent with soot absorption and conversion to [[calcium oxide]], indicating sustained burning above 800°C. One [[limestone]] [[Rosette (design)|rosette]] also preserved a veneer of bone dust fused into its surface through [[vitrification]], though whether the bone was human or animal could not be determined.{{sfn|Shimron|Peleg-Barkat|2010|pp=57–62}}

Evidence for widespread burning and destruction was also uncovered in the [[Jewish Quarter (Jerusalem)|Jewish Quarter]] of the Old City, on the site of the ancient Upper City district. Excavations there were conducted under the direction of archaeologist [[Nahman Avigad]] from 1969 to 1982.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=238–240}} They uncovered evidence of a massive fire and structural collapse, including deposits of ash and building debris up to {{Convert|2|m|ft}} deep in some areas.{{Sfn|Reich|2009|p=34}} The prolonged heat also caused the limestone walls to [[Calcination|calcine]] into calcium oxide.{{Sfn|Reich|2009|p=34}} The fire's effects were also visible on household objects:{{Sfn|Reich|2009|p=34}} [[Stone vessels in ancient Judaea|Limestone vessels]] were stained with ash or calcined into lime, while glass vessels shattered or warped in the heat.{{Sfn|Reich|2009|p=34}} Among the glass finds was a piece made by the renowned first-century glassmaker [[Ennion]], recovered from an Upper City mansion.{{Sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=29}}  The destruction also brought down wooden cellings and upper floors, burying the contents of the buildings beneath the collapse.{{Sfn|Reich|2009|p=34}} Another structure uncovered in the Upper City is the [[Burnt House]]. Excavations there revealed thick layers of ash covering the basement, along with kitchen tools, pottery, and a stone weight inscribed with the name of the priestly Kathros family, suggesting the building may have formed part of the basement of the family villa.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=242–243}} Among the finds were a spear and the skeletal forearm of a young woman who was probably killed during the siege.{{sfn|Geva|2010|pp=60, 66–68}}{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=242–243}}{{Sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=29}}[[File:Fresco from the Second Temple Period, Wohl Archaeological Museum, The Jewish Quarter, Jeruslem.jpg|thumb|A [[fresco]] showing signs of burning, [[Wohl Archaeological Museum]], Jewish Quarter|alt=Faded ancient fresco with red and gray painted wall panels, preserved on a stone wall inside a museum enclosure]]

A large, elaborately constructed building uncovered in the [[Givati Parking Lot dig|Givati Lot]] in the [[City of David (archaeological site)|City of David]],{{Efn|Excavations at the site began in 2007.{{sfn|Ben-Ami|Tchekhanovets|2011|p=231}}}} near the southern edge of the Temple Mount, has been tentatively identified by [[Doron Ben-Ami]] and [[Yana Tchekhanovets]] as one of the Adiabene royal family's palaces in the Lower City. Its occupants had cut narrow, concealable openings into the basement walls, apparently in an attempt to escape shortly before the building's destruction. The structure appears to have been deliberately demolished, with collapsed walls crushing the basement vaults below. Pottery and coins recovered from the debris date the destruction to 70 CE.{{Sfn|Ben-Ami|Tchekhanovets|2011|pp=235, 237, 239}}

Josephus's account of Jerusalem's residents attempting to escape through underground passages after the city's fall aligns with a drainage channel discovered beneath the [[Stepped street (Jerusalem)|stepped street]] in the [[Tyropoeon Valley]]. Excavations revealed the channel was large enough for a person to crawl through and contained intact cooking pots and coins from the revolt.{{sfn|Reich|Shukron|2011|p=253}}{{sfn|Reich|Shukron|pp=82−83|2019}} The great [[Jerusalem Water Channel|urban drainage channel]] and the Pool of Siloam in the Lower City silted up and fell out of use.{{sfn|Weksler-Bdolah|2019|p=3}} Nearby, Ronny Reich and [[Eli Shukron]] uncovered a paved esplanade buried beneath collapsed building stones and debris that had apparently fallen from the City of David hill above.{{sfn|Reich|Shukron|2011|p=246}}{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=260}} Additionally, city walls collapsed in numerous places.{{sfn|Weksler-Bdolah|2019|p=3}}

== Captives and executions ==
After Jerusalem's fall, Titus ordered the killing of those who continued to resist, though many elderly and infirm prisoners were massacred as well, despite his orders to the contrary.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=368}} Survivors in good condition were detained in the [[Court of the Women]], one of the temple's courtyards, where the Roman official Fronto determined their fate. Rebels and brigands were executed, while the tallest and most handsome were selected for Titus's triumph in Rome. Prisoners over 17 were sent in chains to Egypt or distributed across the empire for execution by the sword or [[Damnatio ad bestias|wild animals]], and those under 17 were sold into slavery.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=368–369}}

Around 11,000 prisoners reportedly died of starvation, either from neglect or refusing food. Josephus claims to have rescued his brother and friends, and even intervened to save three men being crucified in [[Teqoa (ancient town)|Teqoa]]—though only one survived.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=368–369}} [[Eusebius]], a 4th-century bishop and historian, writes that Vespasian ordered the eradication of all members of the [[Davidic line]], to prevent any potential Jewish royal resurgence.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=370|ps=, citing Eusebius, ''[[Ecclesiastical History (Eusebius)|Ecclesiastical History]]'', 3.12}}

Once most of Jerusalem's remaining population had been killed or enslaved, the Romans turned to searching the city's underground tunnels for survivors. In several places, paving stones above the tunnels had been deliberately removed and broken, apparently by Roman soldiers searching for people who had taken refuge below.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=370–371}} According to Josephus, many of those found were executed on the spot, while more than 2,000 were discovered already dead, having died from starvation or killed one another.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=370–371}} Soldiers also recovered valuables and prisoners the rebels had been holding. John of Gischala was captured after emerging from the tunnels and was sentenced to [[life imprisonment]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=370–371}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}}

Simon bar Giora was caught after he and his companions, hiding in an underground passage, ran short of food. He surfaced at the site of the destroyed Temple dressed in a white tunic and a purple mantle—a choice of clothing possibly meant to assert a royal claim.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=376–377}}{{sfn|Grabbe|2021|p=421}} The Roman officer Terentius Rufus had him seized and sent to Titus at Caesarea Maritima.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=377}} Both John and Simon were later transported to Rome to be displayed in the triumph.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=130}}

Following the conquest, Titus embarked on a regional victory tour.{{sfn|Davies|2023|p=101}} At [[Banias|Caesarea Philippi]], he staged games featuring Jewish prisoners, including executions by wild animals and [[Gladiator|gladiatorial]] combat. At Caesarea Maritima, some 2,500 captives were killed in similar spectacles to mark his brother's birthday, and further prisoners died during games held in [[Berytus]] in honor of Vespasian's birthday.{{sfn|Beard|2002|p=553}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=376, 377}}

== Aftermath ==
{{Further|First Jewish–Roman War#Aftermath}}

=== Casualties, enslavement, and displacement ===
During the siege of Jerusalem and its aftermath, the population suffered mass extermination.{{Sfn|Kelley|2022|pp=15–17}} Josephus claims that 1.1 million people died in the siege, mostly Jews who came for Passover and became trapped in the city as pilgrims kept arriving despite the revolt.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=369}}{{Sfn|Gruen|2002|p=31|ps=, citing ''War'', 6.420–421}} He also records several waves of desertions before and during the siege.{{sfn|Schwartz|2014b|pp=85–86}} Modern scholars generally regard his death toll as greatly exaggerated. Historian [[Seth Schwartz]], for instance, estimates that [[Roman Palestine]]'s total population at the time was around one million, with roughly half being Jewish. He notes that sizable Jewish communities remained in the region after the war, even in Judea, despite its devastation.{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|p=23}}{{Efn|Schwartz, however, believes that the captive number of 97,000 reported by Josephus is more reliable.{{sfn|Schwartz|2014b|pp=85–86}}}} Guy Rogers estimates the death toll at tens of thousands, likely 20,000–30,000.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=369}} Genocide scholar Shawn J. Kelley has characterized the event as a [[genocide]].{{Sfn|Kelley|2022|pp=15–17}} Gil Gambash, however, argued that since the Romans did not attempt to destroy the Jews as an ''ethnos'' or ''[[genos]]'', but rather directed destruction at communities that resisted them, "[[urbicide]]" may be a more appropriate framework.{{sfn|Gambash|2023|p=341}}

[[File:Jerusalem-Burnt-House-455.jpg|thumb|Layers of ash and collapsed building materials in the basement of a priestly mansion, known today as the [[Burnt House]]|alt=Ancient stone walled underground room with rubble floor, debris and ash, a warm light glowing on one wall, and ceramic jars in the background]]
Many in the surrounding region were also killed, displaced, or enslaved.{{sfn|Schwartz|2014b|pp=85–86}} Josephus reports that after the Romans put the armed men and elderly to death, others were sold into slavery (Josephus gives a total of 97,000 captives taken throughout the war), while 40,000 survivors from Jerusalem were released by the emperor.<ref>{{harvnb|Grabbe|2021|p=421|ps=, ''War'', 6.378–386, 420}}</ref> Traces of this enslaved population surface in Italy: a tombstone from [[Puteoli]], near [[Naples]], commemorates a woman named [[Claudia Aster inscription|Claudia Aster]], identified as a captive from Jerusalem, whose name may derive from [[Esther (given name)|Esther]].{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=133}}{{sfn|Price|2011|p=413}} Roman poet [[Martial]] likewise mentions a Jewish slave in his own household who came from "Jerusalem destroyed by fire."{{sfn|Rocca|2022|pp=272–273}}

=== Triumph and mop-up operations ===
in the summer of 71, about a year after Jerusalem's fall, Titus and Vespasian held a triumph in Rome celebrating their victory in Judaea.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=381}}{{sfn|Huitink|2024|p=216}}{{sfn|Millar|2005|p=101}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=3–5}} This triumph was unique in [[History of the Roman Empire|Roman history]] as the only one dedicated to subjugating the population of an existing province.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=79}}{{sfn|Huitink|2024|p=216}} It is also the best-documented triumph of the imperial era, described in vivid detail by Josephus in Book 7 of ''The Jewish War''.{{sfn|Millar|2005|p=101}}{{sfn|Huitink|2024|p=223}}{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=246}}

At dawn, Vespasian and Titus, wearing [[Laurel wreath|laurel crowns]] and ceremonial attire, left the [[Temple of Isis and Serapis|Temple of Isis]] for the [[Porticus Octaviae]], where they met Rome's leading officials.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=381}} Seated on an [[ivory]] tribunal, they offered prayers of thanksgiving before proceeding to the {{Ill|Porta Triumphalis|it|Porta Triumphalis}}, where they performed [[Animal sacrifice#Ancient Rome|sacrifices]], donned purple [[Toga picta|triumphal robes]], and began the procession.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=381}} The event attracted an enormous crowd, estimated by archaeologist {{Ill|Ernst Künzl|de|Ernst Künzl}} at over 300,000 spectators.{{sfn|Künzl|1988|p=72}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=381}} The procession included 700 Jewish captives, who were paraded "to make a display of their own destruction",{{sfn|Beard|2002|p=553}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=379}} along with sacred items from the Temple, such as the [[Temple menorah|menorah]], the golden [[Showbread|Table of Showbread]], and the [[Torah scroll|scrolls of the Law]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=382, 619}} Vespasian and Titus rode in triumphal chariots, while [[Domitian]], Titus's younger brother, accompanied them on horseback.{{sfn|Beard|2002|p=550}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=383}} Artworks, rugs, gems, statues of the gods, and garlanded animals were carried past on multi-storey floats framed in gold and ivory and hung with tapestries showing scenes from the war.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=381–382}} The triumph culminated on the [[Capitoline Hill]] with the execution of Simon bar Giora at the [[Mamertine Prison]], in accordance with Roman custom.{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=383}}

[[File:Arch_of_Titus_Menorah.png|thumb|In 71, a [[Roman triumph|triumph]] was held in Rome to celebrate Titus's victory. It is commemorated on the [[Arch of Titus]], which depicts Roman soldiers carrying spoils from the Temple, including the [[Temple menorah|menorah]]|upright=1.1|alt=Stone relief depicting Roman soldiers carrying the seven-branched Temple menorah, trumpets, and the Table of the Showbread in a triumphal procession]]After Jerusalem's fall, most of Judaea was finally subdued, with the exception of three strongholds—[[Herodium]], [[Machaerus]], and Masada—which remained under rebel control. Roman [[Legate (ancient Rome)|legates]] were assigned to eliminate these final pockets of resistance.{{sfn|Tropper|2016|pp=91–92}} Herodium and Machaerus fell within two years, leaving Masada as the last stronghold. In 73/74, the Romans breached its walls after a [[Siege of Masada|prolonged siege]], ending the Jewish revolt.{{sfn|Mendels|1992|p=368}}

=== Legio X Fretensis garrisons the ruins of Jerusalem ===
After Jerusalem's fall, Legio X Fretensis, garrisoned the ruins for nearly two centuries.{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=76}}{{sfn|Belayche|2001|pp=82–84}} Its presence is confirmed by inscriptions, tiles, and bricks bearing the legion's stamp, though the camp's exact location remains uncertain.{{sfn|Price|2011|p=414}}{{Efn|It is widely accepted among scholars, however, that it stood on the southwestern hill.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=299}}}} The establishment of a Roman garrison likely discouraged Jews from returning.<ref>{{harvnb|Price|2011|p=414}}</ref> Josephus wrote that Titus granted him land elsewhere because his holdings in Jerusalem were unprofitable with the garrison quartered there.<ref>{{harvnb|Price|2011|p=414}}, citing Josephus, ''[[The Life of Flavius Josephus|Life]]'', 422</ref> He also stated that the Romans cut down all the trees in the vicinity of the city as they constructed their siege works. The once tree-covered and garden-filled countryside became so desolate that those who had known it before would no longer have recognized it.{{sfn|Safrai|1976|p=314}}

[[File:Samuel_and_Saidye_Bronfman_Archaeology_WingDSCN5049.JPG|thumb|A roof tile stamped with the mark of [[Legio X Fretensis]], found near Jerusalem's [[International Convention Center (Jerusalem)|International Convention Center]]|alt=Ancient Roman brick fragment stamped "LEG X F" with a ship above and a boar figure below, mounted on a museum stand]]
A small number of people may have remained after the city's destruction. Josephus has Eleazar ben Yair speak of "hapless old men sit[ting] beside the ashes of the shrine, and a few women, reserved by the enemy for basest outrage".<ref>{{harvnb|Price|2011|p=414}}, citing ''War'', 7.377</ref> [[Epiphanius of Salamis|Epiphanius]], a 4th-century Christian bishop, records what may be authentic testimony of a small, impoverished Jewish community residing on Jerusalem's [[Mount Zion|southwestern hill]] between the revolts.{{sfn|Price|2011|p=415|ps=, Epiphanius, ''[[On Weights and Measures]]'', 129–130}} Excavations at [[Shuafat]], {{Convert|4|km|mi}} north of the Old City, uncovered a post-destruction settlement built in Roman style but inhabited by Jews. It was partially burned and abandoned several decades later, at the start of the [[Bar Kokhba Revolt]].{{sfn|Price|2011|pp=415–416}}

=== Commemoration in Rome ===
To celebrate their triumph, the Flavians initiated a series of grand construction projects in Rome.{{sfn|Goodman|2004|p=17}} In 75, Vespasian completed the [[Temple of Peace, Rome|Temple of Peace]]—a monumental complex dedicated to [[Pax (goddess)|Pax]], the goddess of peace, adjacent to the [[Forum of Augustus]].{{sfn|Rogers|2022|pp=388–389}}{{Sfn|Moormann|2023|p=254}} The temple housed the menorah, the Table of Showbread, and other ritual objects from Jerusalem, along with a large collection of artworks.{{sfn|Goodman|1987|p=236}}{{Sfn|Moormann|2023|p=254}} Another landmark associated with the triumph was the [[Colosseum]], completed under Titus and financed, according to an inscription, "from the spoils of war"—a reference to the war in Judaea.{{sfn|Millar|2005|pp=117–119}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=4}}

The Flavians also launched a coinage campaign known as {{lang|la|[[Judaea Capta coinage|Judaea capta]]}} ("Judaea has been conquered").{{sfn|Magness|2012|pp=166–167}} Issued over a span of 10 to 12 years, these coins became a central tool of Flavian propaganda.{{sfn|Overman|2002|p=215}} The obverse featured Vespasian or Titus,{{sfn|Overman|2002|p=215}} while the reverse depicted a mourning female figure, symbolizing the subjugated Jewish people, seated beneath a palm tree, the emblem of Judaea.{{sfn|Magness|2012|pp=166–167}} In some variations, the figure appears bound or kneeling before the victory goddess [[Nike (mythology)|Nike]] ([[Victoria (deity)|Victoria]]).{{sfn|Overman|2002|p=215}}

[[File:Roma_Arco_di_Titus10.jpg|thumb|upright=1.15|The [[Arch of Titus]], built c. 81 on the [[Via Sacra]], is one of the monumental structures in Rome commemorating the victory|alt=Ancient Roman triumphal arch with a Latin inscription, standing on a paved plaza with tourists walking below]]
The emperor [[Domitian]] (r. 81–96) continued to commemorate the victory, commissioning two [[triumphal arch]]es that glorified the Flavian dynasty and its military success.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=266}}{{sfn|Overman|2002|pp=217–218}} Most prominent is the [[Arch of Titus]], still standing along the [[Via Sacra]], ancient Rome's main thoroughfare.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|pp=261–262}}{{sfn|Goodman|1987|p=236}} Built shortly after Titus's death, the arch was dedicated by the [[Senate and People of Rome]] to the deified Titus.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|pp=261–262}} Its interior reliefs portray soldiers parading the seven-branched menorah, the Table of Showbread, a golden cup, and silver trumpets, during the triumphal procession.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=131}}{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=263}} On the opposite panel, Titus appears in a [[quadriga]], crowned with a laurel wreath by Victoria, while [[Virtus (deity)|Virtus]], the personification of bravery and military strength, leads the chariot.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|p=263}} The menorah depicted on the arch was later chosen as the [[emblem of Israel]].{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=131}} A [[Arch of Titus (Circus Maximus)|second triumphal arch]], constructed c. 81,{{sfn|Overman|2002|p=217}} stood near the southeastern edge of the [[Circus Maximus]], Rome's main [[chariot racing]] venue.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|pp=265–266}}{{sfn|Rogers|2022|p=4}} Its inscription proclaimed that Titus "subdued the Jewish people and destroyed the city of Jerusalem, a feat either sought in vain by all generals, kings, and peoples before him or entirely untried," overlooking earlier conquests of the city.{{sfn|Rocca|2022|pp=265–266}}{{sfn|Overman|2002|pp=217–218}}
=== Aelia Capitolina and the Bar Kokhba Revolt ===
{{main|Aelia Capitolina|Bar Kokhba Revolt}}
In 129/130, the emperor [[Hadrian]] visited Judaea and founded the Roman colony of [[Aelia Capitolina]] on Jerusalem's ruins — a move widely regarded as a key trigger for the Bar Kokhba Revolt (132–136).{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=338–339}}{{sfn|Goodman|1987|pp=27–28}} The rebels established a short-lived independent Jewish state before it was crushed by Rome, leaving widespread destruction and depopulation across Judea proper. Hadrian renamed the province from Judaea to [[Syria Palaestina]] and barred Jews from entering Jerusalem and its vicinity.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=295–296}}{{sfn|Price|2011|p=414}} Aelia Capitolina itself grew into a modest town devoid of Jerusalem's former Jewish character. Its population included Roman legionaries and non-Jewish settlers, and its buildings included [[Roman temple|temples]] dedicated to [[Religion in ancient Rome|Roman deities]], in contrast to the Temple that had once dominated the city.{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=338–339}}

For the following five centuries, Roman authorities permitted Jews to enter Jerusalem only once a year, on [[Tisha B'Av]] (Ninth of Av), to mourn the Temple's destruction. A [[Itinerarium Burdigalense|Christian pilgrim from Bordeaux]] who visited the city in 333 recorded that Jews would come annually to anoint a [[Foundation Stone|perforated stone]], then "bewail themselves with groans, rend their garments, and so depart."{{sfn|Friedman|1996|p=138}} Under the emperor [[Julian (emperor)|Julian]] (r. 361–363), Jews were briefly permitted to return, and may have begun efforts to rebuild the Temple, but the project was halted by a natural disaster (possibly an [[363 Galilee earthquake|earthquake]]) and ended for good with Julian's death shortly afterward.{{sfn|Friedman|1996|p=137}} Apart from this episode and another brief respite during the short-lived [[Sasanian conquest of Jerusalem|Sasanian conquest]], Jews were not permitted to resettle in Jerusalem until after the [[Siege of Jerusalem (636–637)|Muslim conquest]] in the 630s.{{sfn|Friedman|1996|pp=138–139}} The Temple Mount itself appears to have remained largely in ruins, until the [[Umayyad Caliphate|Umayyad]] caliph [[Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan|Abd al-Malik]] built the [[Dome of the Rock]] there in the 690s.{{sfn|Silberman|2002|p=240}}

=== Fate of the temple treasures ===
While most of the Temple treasures were publicly displayed in Rome's Temple of Peace,{{sfn|Goodman|1987|p=236}}{{sfn|Moormann|2023|p=254}} items such as the [[Parochet|purple curtains]] and a [[Torah scroll]] were instead deposited within the imperial palace.{{sfn|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}} Rabbinic tradition attributed to [[Eliezer ben Jose]] (possibly dated to c. 170 CE) recounts that he saw a golden [[diadem]] and the curtain in Rome, possibly in Vespasian's private treasury. A slightly later source mentions these objects along with the menorah and the Table of Showbread.{{sfn|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}}

In the 6th century, the [[Byzantines|Byzantine]] historian [[Procopius]] recorded that the Jewish treasures Titus had carried off from Jerusalem were among the trophies paraded in [[Constantinople]] in 534 after the [[Vandalic War]].<ref>{{harvnb|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}}, citing [[Procopius]], ''De Bello Vandalico'', 4.9.4</ref> He states that these had previously been taken to Carthage by the [[Vandal Kingdom|Vandal]] king [[Gaiseric]], after his [[Sack of Rome (455)|sack of Rome]] in 455. During the procession, a Jewish onlooker reportedly warned that these objects belonged only in the place originally designated by King [[Solomon]], and that their possession had empowered both Gaiseric and now [[Belisarius]] in conquest. Alarmed by the warning, the [[Byzantine Empire|Byzantine]] emperor [[Justinian I|Justinian]] ordered that the items be sent to Christian shrines in Jerusalem.{{sfn|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}} Some later medieval sources claimed that the treasures remained in Rome. The seven-branched lamp depicted in the [[apse]] mosaic of [[Santi Cosma e Damiano, Rome|Santi Cosma e Damiano]] in the city has been associated by art historian John Osborne with the menorah.{{sfn|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}} If the objects remained in Jerusalem after Justinian sent them there, they were likely lost during the [[Sasanian conquest of Jerusalem|Sasanian sack of the city]] in 614.{{sfn|Moormann|2023|pp=256–257}}

== Jewish responses ==
The destruction of the Second Temple marked a turning point in [[Jewish history]], transforming Jewish identity and practice.{{sfn|Gruen|2002|pp=18–19}} With the loss of its religious and national center, Judaism had to adapt to displacement and uncertainty.{{sfn|Gruen|2002|pp=18–19}} The collapse of the Temple cult brought an end to the High Priesthood,{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=70}} rendered sacrificial rites impossible,{{sfn|Millar|1995|p=70}}{{sfn|Cohen|1984|p=27}} and led to the decline of sectarianism.{{sfn|Cohen|1984|pp=31, 35–36}} In response, new forms of worship and religious practice emerged.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=136}} The [[Pharisees]], one of the leading sects of late [[Second Temple Judaism]], emerged as the central influence reshaping Jewish religious life.{{sfn|Schaper|1999|pp=426–427}} Their tradition gave rise to the [[Rabbi|rabbinic]] movement, which maintained close connections with the Pharisees without being exclusively Pharisaic, adopting the broader identity of "[[Chazal|sages]] of Israel".{{sfn|Cohen|2014|pp=224–225}} This movement emphasized [[Torah study]], [[Jewish prayer|communal prayer]], and [[Chesed|acts of loving-kindness]],{{Sfn|Safrai|1976|p=318}}{{sfn|Bokser|1983|p=61}} inaugurating a new era in the absence of both the Temple and Jewish sovereignty.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=136}}

The Temple's destruction prompted theological reflection. Drawing on the precedent of [[Siege of Jerusalem (587 BC)|Jerusalem's destruction]] by the [[Neo-Babylonian Empire|Babylonians]] in 586/587 BCE, many Jews saw their suffering as a divine consequence of [[Jewish ethics|moral]] or religious transgressions.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=196}} The idea that exile resulted from disobedience but that repentance could restore divine favor had been reinforced when the [[Achaemenid Empire|Persian]] king [[Cyrus the Great|Cyrus]] allowed exiled Jews to [[Return to Zion|return and rebuild the temple]] c. 538 BCE.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=196}} However, while the Second Temple was rebuilt within some seventy years of the destruction of the First, the Romans did not allow a similar reconstruction after its destruction, leaving Jewish expectations unfulfilled.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=194}}

=== Apocalyptic literature ===
The destruction prompted a resurgence of [[Jewish Apocalypticism|Jewish apocalyptic]] literature, which sought to interpret the destruction of the Temple and expressed hopes for its restoration.{{sfn|Alexander|2024|p=205}}{{sfn|Kirschner|1985|pp=27–28}} Two apocalypses of the period drew on the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem,{{sfn|Jones|2011|p=89}}{{sfn|Kirschner|1985|pp=29–30}} an event already central to the [[Hebrew Bible]], as a framework for understanding the more recent catastrophe.{{sfn|Jones|2011|p=75}}{{Quote box

One such work is [[2 Baruch]],{{Efn|This text was preserved in [[Syriac alphabet|Syriac]], but widely believed to have been originally composed in Hebrew, likely in the late first century CE.{{sfn|Jones|2011|pp=87–89}}}} a [[Jewish apocrypha and pseudepigrapha|pseudepigraphal text]] ascribed to [[Baruch ben Neriah]]. Baruch was a scribe of Jeremiah who lived at the time of the First Temple's destruction, though it was actually composed after the Second Temple's fall. It begins with God telling Baruch that Jerusalem is about to fall because of the people's sins, and instructing him to warn others to flee. Baruch protests that he would rather die than see the city taken and that its destruction will erase Israel's legacy; God replies that the true Jerusalem is preserved in heaven, untouched. Baruch then calls on both humanity and the natural world to join the mourning. The work ends with Baruch urging those left in the land to keep the Law, and writing to the exiles in [[Assyrian captivity|Assyria]] and [[Babylonian captivity|Babylonia]] to remain faithful as they await redemption.{{sfn|Jones|2011|pp=86–87}}

[[4 Ezra]], a work dated to the reign of [[Domitian]] (81–96),{{sfn|Jones|2011|pp=44, 56}} features a series of dialogues between the biblical scribe [[Ezra]] and the angel [[Uriel]].{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|p=32}}{{sfn|Jones|2011|p=42}} Ezra is deeply shaken upon witnessing "the desolation of Zion and the wealth of those who lived in [[Babylon]]"; he asks why should Israel remain faithful when obedience has brought ruin, and why do its oppressors flourish.{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|pp=31–32}} Uriel's answer, that Ezra himself is [[Righteousness#Judaism|righteous]] and will be rewarded, leaves him unpersuaded.{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|p=31}} The second half turns to visions of the [[Eschatology|end of days]].{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|p=32}}{{Efn|Some scholars interpret these visions as a resolution to Ezra's doubts.{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|p=32}} In one of these visions, Ezra sees an eagle with twelve large wings, eight smaller ones, and three heads; as its parts vanish one by one, a lion rebukes it, and it is consumed by flames. The angel explains that the eagle represents the [[Four kingdoms of Daniel|fourth kingdom of Daniel]]'s vision, while the lion symbolizes the [[Messiah in Judaism|Messiah]] bringing judgment (4 Ezra 11:1–12:36).{{sfn|Jones|2011|p=42}} In another vision, Ezra is commissioned to restore the Law, first warning the people that their suffering comes from disobedience, and then dictating 94 books, with 24 made public and 70 reserved for the wise.{{sfn|Jones|2011|p=42}}}} The text holds that divine justice, though not immediately visible, will ultimately be revealed when [[Gathering of Israel|Israel is restored]] and its enemies punished. Rome is destined to fall by divine judgment through the [[Messiah in Judaism|Messiah]], a future liberator of the Jewish people.{{sfn|Jones|2011|pp=76–77}} One of the [[Sibylline Oracles]], a Jewish prophecy from the post-destruction era, frames the [[Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD|eruption of Mount Vesuvius]] in 79 as divine punishment for Jerusalem's destruction.{{Sfn|Grabbe|2021|pp=64–65|ps=, citing Oracle 4.115–136}}

=== The destruction in rabbinic literature ===
Rabbinic literature engages with the fall of Jerusalem through stories, traditions, [[Midrash|exegeses]], and teachings that developed over the centuries.{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=231}} The rabbis attributed it to internal causes—factional conflict, the misuse of wealth, failures of leadership, neglect of communal responsibility, and [[Jewish views on sin|sin]].{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|pp=222, 229}} This interpretation followed the biblical tradition that Jerusalem's divine protection was forfeited through Israel's transgressions, with Rome's victory understood as punishment.{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=232}}

Early rabbinic works, composed by the ''[[Tannaim]]''—the [[Chazal|sages]] active from Jerusalem's destruction until the early 3rd century—reflect deep sorrow and anguish over the loss of the Temple.{{Sfn|Safrai|1976|p=318}} The ''[[Mishnah]]'' laments that with the destruction, "faithful men came to an end", and since that time, "there has been no day without its curse". Similarly, ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' states that the world was blessed while the Temple service continued, and that blessing departed once it ceased.{{Sfn|Leaney|1984|p=120|ps=, citing ''[[Mishnah]], [[Sotah (Talmud)|Sotah]]'', 9:12 and ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' (Version B), 5}}

This comes to teach you that the sin of wanton hatred is equivalent to the three severe transgressions: Idol worship, forbidden sexual relations and bloodshed.

The Babylonian Talmud (''Gittin'' 55b–57a) provides an extensive narrative detailing the city's destruction and the factors that led to it.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=204}}{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=229}} Another account, rich with interpretive [[Homily|homilies]], appears in Lamentations Rabbah, particularly in its opening sections.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=204}} The ''[[Yoma]]'' tractate of the Babylonian Talmud explains that the Second Temple fell due to the grave sin of baseless hatred ({{lang|he|שִׂנְאַת חִנָּם}}; {{tlit|he|sin'at ḥinnam}}).{{sfn|Price|1992|pp=178–179}}

One of the most famous Talmudic tales explaining the destruction is the story of [[Kamsa and Bar Kamsa]]. In this account, a wealthy man in Jerusalem mistakenly invited his enemy, Bar Kamsa, to a banquet instead of his friend, Kamsa. When Bar Kamsa arrived, he was publicly humiliated and expelled despite offering to pay for the entire feast, while the attending sages remained silent. Seeking revenge, Bar Kamsa told the Roman emperor that the Jews had rebelled against Rome, triggering a chain of events that ultimately led to war.{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=230}}{{efn|According to the story, Bar Kamsa told the emperor that the Jews had rebelled and suggested he test their loyalty by sending an offering to the Temple. The emperor dispatched a calf with Bar Kamsa, who secretly inflicted a blemish on it, one that rendered it unfit for sacrifice under Jewish law. The priests then debated whether to sacrifice the animal anyway, to avoid offending Rome, or to kill Bar Kamsa to keep him from reporting the refusal; Rabbi [[Zechariah ben Abkilus]] objected to both proposals, and neither was carried out. The narrative holds his excessive scrupulousness responsible for the Temple's destruction.}} Another tale describes how three wealthy men—Ben Kalba Sabua, [[Nicodemus ben Gurion|Naqdimon ben Gurion]], and Ben Sisit Hakkeset—had stockpiled enough to sustain the city for twenty-one years; the revolutionaries ([[Jewish Babylonian Aramaic]]: {{tlit|tmr|baryoni}}) burned the stores to make surrender impossible and force the city to fight.{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=230}}

Rabbinic literature refers to Titus as "Titus the Wicked",{{sfn|Fine|2023|p=547}} portraying him as arrogant and blasphemous, and emphasizing that although Israel's enemies may gain power, they serve only as instruments of divine wrath destined for punishment.{{sfn|Yavetz|1975|p=414}} One legend recounts that Titus desecrated the [[Holy of Holies]], the Temple's innermost chamber, by having intercourse with a prostitute on a Torah scroll before slashing the [[Parochet|veil]] with his sword, causing it to bleed.{{sfn|Yavetz|1975|p=413}} The legend continues that a gnat flew into Titus's nose and gnawed at his brain until he died.<ref>{{harvnb|Fine|2023|pp=547–548, 560–561}}</ref>{{efn|These legends speak of a "Yattush", which may refer to either a mosquito or a [[gnat]].{{sfn|Fine|2023|p=544}} According to the Babylonian Talmud, the creature grew in his head for seven years before he died.{{sfn|Fine|2023|pp=547–548, 560–561|ps=, citing Babylonian Talmud, ''Gittin'', 56b}}{{sfn|Yavetz|1975|pp=413–414}} According to ''[[Genesis Rabbah]]'', an autopsy after his death revealed a two-pound bird inside his head, while ''[[Leviticus Rabbah]]'' recounts that Titus requested a procedure to determine how he had been punished by the God of Israel.{{sfn|Fine|2023|pp=560–561|ps=, citing ''Genesis Rabbah'', 10:7 and ''Leviticus Rabbah'', 2:502}}}}

Rabbinic literature records visits to Jerusalem's ruins.<ref>{{harvnb|Price|2011|p=415}}</ref> One account describes [[Rabbi Akiva]] and three colleagues coming up to the ruined Temple Mount and seeing a fox emerge from the place where the Holy of Holies had stood. The others wept; Akiva laughed, explaining that now that [[Uriah (prophet)|Uriah]]'s prophecy of desolation had been fulfilled, [[Zechariah (Hebrew prophet)|Zechariah]]'s prophecy of Jerusalem's restoration would be fulfilled too.{{sfn|Levine|2018|p=167|ps=, citing [[Sifre|''Sifre to Deuteronomy'']] 43; Babylonian Talmud, ''[[Makkot]]'' 24a–b}} Additional rabbinic texts portray God, along with [[Moses]], the [[Patriarchs (Bible)|patriarchs]], prophets, and [[Angels in Judaism|angels]], as mourning Jerusalem's destruction.{{sfn|Saldarini|2002|p=222}}{{sfn|Price|1992|p=178}}

=== Ben Zakkai, Yavneh, and the emergence of the Rabbinic movement ===
According to rabbinic sources,{{efn|The episode is referenced in five works: ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' (Versions A and B), [[Lamentations Rabbah|Midrash Lamentations]], the [[Babylonian Talmud]] (Gittin), and [[Midrash Proverbs]], with notable differences in the traditions.{{sfn|Alon|1977|pp=296–297}}{{sfn|Safrai|1976|pp=319–320}}}} the prominent sage [[Yohanan ben Zakkai|Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai]] ({{tlit|he|Ribaz}}) was hidden in a coffin and smuggled out of Jerusalem during the siege.{{sfn|Magness|2024|p=194}} After meeting Vespasian and prophesying his rise to the throne,{{Efn|According to legend, Ben Zakkai quoted a prophecy from [[Book of Isaiah|Isaiah]] ([[Isaiah 10|10]]:34): "And the Lebanon shall fall by a majestic one." In this context, "Lebanon" is understood to refer to the temple, constructed from the [[Cedrus libani|cedars of Lebanon]], while "majestic one" is interpreted as referring to Vespasian.{{sfn|Cohen|2014|p=24}}}} he secured the establishment of a rabbinic center in [[Yavne]]h. From there, he and his disciples laid the groundwork for the transformation of Jerusalem.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=139}} A passage in ''Avot de-Rabbi Natan'' illustrates this shift, recounting how Ben Zakkai consoled his disciple, [[Joshua ben Hananiah]], by teaching that acts of loving-kindness are equivalent to the temple's sacrificial atonement.{{sfn|Bokser|1983|p=38}}

The rabbinic approach called for restrained mourning that did not disrupt daily life.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=199}} Some Jews adopted [[Asceticism|ascetic practices]],{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=199}} while others retreated to caves for fasting and awaited redemption.{{sfn|Cohen|1999|p=315}}{{Sfn|Safrai|1976|p=318}} A debate in the ''[[Tosefta]]'' ''Sotah'' and Babylonian Talmud (''[[Bava Batra]]'' 60b) recounts Rabbi Joshua countering those advocating abstention from meat and wine due to their role in temple offerings.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=199}} He argued this logic would require abandoning bread, fruit, and water, leaving his opponents speechless.{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=199}} He also reminded them of the rabbinic enactment to leave a small section unpainted when plastering a new home as a memorial for Jerusalem.{{sfn|Cohen|1999|p=315}}

"My son," Rabban Johanan said to him, "be not grieved; we have another atonement as effective as this. And what is it? It is acts of loving-kindness, as it is said, "For I desire mercy and not sacrifice" ([[Book of Hosea|Hosea]] 6:6).

The period after the Temple's destruction saw {{tlit|he|Ribaz}} assume a leading role in reshaping Judaism.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|p=139}} He is credited with introducing several enactments ({{tlit|he|taqqanot}}) which adapted Jewish religious practices to function in the absence of the Temple.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|pp=140–141}}{{sfn|Levine|2005|p=199}}{{Efn|Such enactments appear in Mishnah, Rosh Hashanah 4:1–4{{sfn|Cohen|1999|p=315}}}} Among these, it was decreed that if [[Rosh Hashanah]] fell on a Shabbat, the {{tlit|he|[[shofar]]}} could be blown in any location with a [[Beth din|court]], rather than only in the Temple.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|pp=140–141}} Similarly, during [[Sukkot]], the {{tlit|he|[[lulav]]}} was permitted to be carried outside Jerusalem for all seven days of the festival.{{sfn|Schäfer|2003|pp=140–141}} Jewish prayer was also formalized, with the ''[[Amidah]]'' ("the Standing Prayer") becoming a central prayer recited three times daily,{{sfn|Levine|2005|p=200}} its timing linked to the Temple sacrifices.{{sfn|Bokser|1983|pp=46–47}}{{efn|The ''Mishnah'' (''Berakhot 1.1'') and ''Tosefta'' (''Berakhot'' 3.1–3) acknowledged a correlation between prayer and sacrificial offerings but did not equate the two; [[Amoraim|Amoraic]] sources first introduce prayer as a substitute (Jerusalem Talmud, ''Berakhot'' 4, 1, 7b; Babylonian Talmud, ''Berakhot'' 26b), a notion later expanded to portray it, along with other rituals, as superior.{{sfn|Bokser|1983|pp=46–47, 57}}}} The priestly class maintained its influence by contributing to synagogue liturgy and possibly to biblical translations.{{sfn|Meyers|Chancey|2012|p=164}} The establishment of the center at Yavneh fostered a structured and authoritative system of rabbinic scholarship,{{sfn|DeSilva|2024|p=162}} shaping Jewish life through the integration of [[Oral Torah|oral tradition]] with the [[written Torah]]. These efforts later led to the compilation of the ''Mishnah'' and the two Talmuds, which became central sources of Jewish law and guidance.{{sfn|Stuckenbruck|Gurtner|2019|pp=694–695, 834–836}}{{sfn|Cohen|2014|pp=227–228}}

== Legacy and cultural impact ==

=== In Jewish tradition and culture ===
{{See also|Jerusalem in Judaism}}
The destruction of Jerusalem marked a profound turning point for the Jewish people, and Jewish political control over the city would not resume until the 20th century.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|pp=39–40}}{{Efn|Jewish political control over part of Jerusalem returned with the [[establishment of Israel]] in 1948. Between 1948 and 1967, the city was divided, with Israel controlling the western part and [[Jordan]] the [[East Jerusalem|eastern part]], including the Old City. The Jordanian-held areas came under Israeli control during the [[Six-Day War|1967 war]].{{sfn|Magness|2024|pp=240, 370}}}} Even so, Jerusalem remained central to Jewish religious life and identity throughout this long period. Hope for Jerusalem's restoration and renewed Jewish nationhood persisted in [[Jewish literature|literature]], prayer, [[Jewish music|song]], and [[Jewish art|art]].{{Sfn|Levine|2008|pp=39–40}}{{sfn|Friedman|1996|pp=136–137, 145–146}}

[[File:PikiWiki_Israel_44784_Tisha_BAv_at_the_Western_Wall.JPG|thumb|upright=1.1|Jews praying at the [[Western Wall]], a remnant of the retaining walls of the Temple Mount, during [[Tisha B'Av]], a fast day commemorating the destruction of the Temples|alt=Dozens of Jewish worshippers praying, reading from books, and sitting on the plaza before the Western Wall in Jerusalem during Tisha B'Av, with the massive limestone blocks of the wall filling the background]]
In Judaism, the destruction of the Second Temple is commemorated on [[Tisha B'Av]], a major [[Ta'anit|fast day]] that also marks the destruction of the First Temple, along with other catastrophic events in Jewish history, including the fall of [[Betar (ancient city)|Betar]] and the [[expulsion of Jews from Spain]].{{sfn|Daschke|2010|pp=1–2}} On this day, special {{tlit|he|[[kinnot|qinnot]]}} (Hebrew [[Dirge|dirges]]) are recited as part of the prayer service, and the [[Book of Lamentations]] is read.{{sfn|Rand|2007|pp=131–132}} For [[The Three Weeks|three weeks]] leading up to this day, special prophetic readings are recited in the synagogue, and practices such as weddings, haircuts, and eating meat are prohibited during the first eight days of Av.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|p=42}} The Western Wall,{{efn|It has sometimes been referred to as the "Wailing Wall" due to the lamentations historically performed there.{{sfn|Daschke|2010|pp=1–2}}}} the most substantial remnant of the Second Temple, has long served as a focal point for Jewish prayer and mourning, symbolizing both the loss of the Jewish homeland and hopes for its restoration.{{sfn|Daschke|2010|pp=1–2}}

[[File:שבירת_כוס.jpg|thumb|upright=0.85|A groom breaks a glass underfoot during a [[Jewish wedding]] ceremony, a symbolic act commemorating the Temple's destruction|alt=Close-up of a groom's foot raised above a cloth-wrapped glass on the floor, with the bride's white lace dress beside him]]
The destruction is also recalled in lifecycle rituals. At [[Jewish wedding|Jewish weddings]], the groom breaks a glass to commemorate the Temple's destruction, often accompanied by a recitation of [[Psalm 137]]:5–6: "If I forget you, O Jerusalem, Let my right hand wither."{{Sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=203}}{{Sfn|Levine|2008|pp=42–43}} Other mourning traditions include leaving a section of a home unpainted or refraining from wearing a full set of jewellery on joyous occasions.{{Sfn|Goldenberg|2006|p=203}} In late antiquity, some Jewish communities began dating life events from the Temple's destruction.{{sfn|Sivan|2008|pp=243–245}}{{efn|In [[Zoara]], south of the Dead Sea, this system was regularly used in the Jewish cemetery. One inscription, for a woman named Marsa, records her death as occurring on "17 Elul, the fourth year of <i>[[shemitah]]</i>, 362 years after the temple's destruction."{{sfn|Sivan|2008|pp=243–245}}}} A common expression of comfort during funerary customs, both in the cemetery and afterward, is "May God comfort you among the mourners of Zion and Jerusalem."{{sfn|Levine|2008|p=43}} "[[L'Shana Haba'ah|Next Year in Jerusalem]]" is a recurring declaration during various points of the Hebrew calendar, notably at the [[Passover Seder]].{{sfn|Levine|2008|p=42}}

Jews, both individually and in groups, attempted to return to Jerusalem throughout history, with notable cases including [[Judah Halevi]] in the 12th century and the followers of [[Sabbatai Zevi]] in the 17th century.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|p=44}} The Talmuds, compiled in the 4th and 5th centuries, provide detailed prescriptions for mourning rituals observed by pilgrims visiting Jerusalem, including guidelines for tearing garments and reciting prayers when witnessing the ruins of Judea, Jerusalem, and the Temple.{{sfn|Friedman|1996|p=138}} ''[[Lekha Dodi]]'', a central [[Piyyut|''piyyut'']] (liturgical poem) recited during the welcoming of [[Shabbat]], was composed in 16th-century [[Safed]] and devotes five of its nine stanzas to Jerusalem, mourning its destruction and expressing hope for its restoration.{{Sfn|Levine|2008|p=41}} The [[Third Temple|rebuilding of the Temple]] and the restoration of sacrifices remain central themes in [[Orthodox Judaism|Orthodox Jewish]] liturgy.{{Sfn|Marx|2013|pp=62, 67–69}}

Jewish diaspora communities preserved legends of their ancestors' exile from Jerusalem. A tradition recorded among [[Sephardic Jews|Jews of Spain]] holds that their ancestors were taken there following the city's fall to Titus, with the earliest reference appearing in [[Seder Olam Zutta|Seder Olam Zuta]] (c. 800 CE).{{sfn|Bowers|1975|p=399}} One tradition holds that Jerusalemite exiles named the city of [[Toledo, Spain|Toledo]], linking it to the Hebrew words {{tlit|he|toledot}} or {{tlit|he|tulaytula}}, meaning "migration" or "wandering".{{sfn|Gerber|2021||pp=|p=166}} The medieval Abu Albalia family traced its ancestry to Baruch, a skilled [[silk]] weaver who, according to their tradition, was sent by Titus to [[Mérida, Spain|Mérida]] along with other noble Jerusalemite families at the request of the local governor.{{sfn|Landsberger|1941|pp=340–341}} Similar traditions appear in [[Italy in the Middle Ages|medieval Italian]] sources. The eleventh-century ''[[Chronicle of Ahimaaz]]'' and later manuscripts of ''[[Josippon]]'' recount that around 5,000 captives taken by Titus were relocated to various cities in [[Apulia]], including [[Oria, Apulia|Oria]], [[Otranto]], and [[Trani]].{{sfn|Von Falkenhausen|2012|pp=273, 282}}

The memory of the destruction continues to resonate in modern [[Israeli culture]]. Accepting the [[Nobel Prize in Literature|Nobel Prize]] in 1966, Israeli author [[Shmuel Yosef Agnon]] told the audience that he had been born in one of the cities of the Exile because Titus had destroyed Jerusalem, but that he had always considered himself "one who was born in Jerusalem."{{sfn|Agnon|1967|p=6}} Anthropologist [[Nadia Abu El Haj|Nadia Abu El-Haj]] has argued that the Burnt House and Herodian Quarter emphasize the "splendor and destruction" of Herodian Jerusalem within a broader Israeli national-historical discourse, connecting the ancient city to the modern reconstruction of the Jewish Quarter.{{Sfn|Abu El-Haj|2001|pp=206–209}}

The destruction has also featured in [[Zionism|Zionist]] and Israeli political rhetoric. In December 1918, reflecting on the foundation ceremony of the [[Hebrew University of Jerusalem|Hebrew University]] on Mount Scopus several months earlier, [[Chaim Weizmann]], then a leading Zionist statesman and later Israel's first [[President of Israel|president]], wrote: "Here we are, on the very mountain from which Titus destroyed Jerusalem, laying the foundation-stone of this institution which will rebuild a regenerated Judaea."{{Sfn|Renton|2007|pp=119–120}} In December 1949, days after the [[United Nations General Assembly|UN General Assembly]] reiterated that Jerusalem should be placed under [[Corpus separatum (Jerusalem)|international administration]], Israel's first prime minister [[David Ben-Gurion]] told the [[Knesset]] that Israel would not accept the loss of its sovereignty over the city. He stated that the Jewish people had twice been driven from Jerusalem, first by Babylon and later by Rome, yet argued that the nation's bond with the city remained "no less deep than in the days of Nebuchadnezzar and Titus Flavius." He added that those who had defended Jerusalem during the [[1948 Arab–Israeli War]] had shown the same courage as their ancestors during the Temple periods, and declared that after twenty-five centuries of remaining faithful to Jerusalem, the nation would not give it up. Days later, the Knesset and [[Cabinet of Israel|government]] relocated to Jerusalem.{{Sfn|Avi-Hai|2020}}

=== In Christian theology ===
[[File:Brooklyn_Museum_-_The_Prophecy_of_the_Destruction_of_the_Temple_(La_prédication_de_la_ruine_du_Temple)_-_James_Tissot.jpg|thumb|[[Early Christianity|Early Christians]] interpreted the destruction of the Temple as the fulfillment of [[Jesus]]'s prophecy, as recorded in the Gospels. "The Prophecy of the Destruction of the Temple", painting by [[James Tissot]]|alt=Watercolor painting by James Tissot of Jesus Christ seated under a large tree, head resting in his hand in a posture of grief, overlooking the ancient walled city of Jerusalem in the distance, with four disciples seated nearby on the grassy hillside]]In some early Christian texts, the destruction of Jerusalem was depicted as divine punishment for the Jewish people's [[rejection of Jesus]], drawing on the prophetic tradition in which sin brings [[divine retribution]].{{sfn|Schwartz|2006|pp=24, 30–31}}{{sfn|Goldenberg|2006|pp=197–198}} The [[Gospel]]s—beginning with the [[Gospel of Mark]], composed c. 70—contain prophecies attributed to Jesus foretelling the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple ([[Mark 13]], [[Matthew 24]], [[Luke 21]]), with the [[Gospel of Matthew]] possibly alluding to the burning of the city.{{sfn|Mitchell|2006|p=188}} The [[Epistle of Barnabas]] (written between 70 and 135) framed the destruction as evidence that God had rejected the physical Temple in favor of a new, spiritual one, embodied in the faith and conversion of [[Gentile]] believers.{{sfn|Lieu|2006|p=219}} [[Justin Martyr]], writing after 135, interpreted the Temple's destruction as punishment for the [[crucifixion of Jesus]], and viewed the second revolt as sealing Jerusalem's desolation—a sign, in his view, that the Temple cult and [[Covenant (biblical)|God's covenant]] with the Jewish people were temporary and had now been [[Supersessionism|superseded by the Church]].{{sfn|Clements|2012|p=533}}

According to 4th-century Church Fathers Eusebius and Epiphanius, the Christian community in Jerusalem [[Flight to Pella|fled to Pella]] in Transjordan after receiving divine instruction, thereby avoiding the city's destruction.{{sfn|Paget|2018|p=279}}{{sfn|Mitchell|2006|p=298}} The account has been questioned because of its late attestation, its mention of a divine warning, and the fact that [[Pella, Jordan|Pella]] itself was attacked by Jewish rebels during the war. Some scholars suggest the community simply fled as others did; others that they surrendered to Rome and were resettled at Pella.{{sfn|Paget|2018|pp=279–280}}

By the late 4th century, Christian writers reinforced these theological readings. [[John Chrysostom]] told his congregation that Christ, after being crucified, had destroyed the Jews' city, dispersed their people, and scattered the nation across the earth — proof, he argued, that Jesus was risen and reigning.{{sfn|Kessler|2010|p=60|ps=, citing [[John Chrysostom]], ''Adversus Iudaeos'', 5.7}} [[Jerome]] described Jews paying for the right to enter the city once a year and mourning amid its ruins — ragged old men and wailing women, in his telling, displaying God's wrath in their very bodies and appearance, while the cross gleamed from the Mount of Olives.{{sfn|Millar|2010|pp=65, 77|ps=, citing [[Jerome]], ''Commentary on Zephaniah'' 1:15–16}}{{efn|Theological interpretations persisted into the modern period. In the 1890s, [[Brooke Foss Westcott]], [[Bishop of Durham]], called Jerusalem's fall "the most significant national event" in world history, arguing that once the "more perfect [[Tabernacle]]" (i.e., Jesus) had been rejected, the temple was "necessarily doomed to final desolation."{{sfn|Knight|1896|pp=9–10}}}}
=== In art, literature and popular culture ===
Jerusalem's fall has inspired writers and artists since antiquity. An early medieval depiction appears on the [[Franks Casket]], an 8th-century whalebone box from [[History of Anglo-Saxon England|Anglo-Saxon England]]. Its back panel portrays the city's capture in a way seemingly informed by Josephus.{{Sfn|McKitterick|Ward|2018|pp=231–233}} The scene is labeled with the words "judgement" and "hostage", alongside an inscription interpreted as: "Here Titus and the Jews fight. Here its inhabitants flee from Jerusalem."{{Sfn|McKitterick|Ward|2018|pp=231–233}} Medieval Christian literature developed legends in which Vespasian and Titus were depicted as Christian heroes who converted after miraculous healings by [[Saint Veronica]]'s relic. Their conversion was followed by their taking vengeance against the Jewish people.{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=3–4}}{{sfn|Zissos|2016|pp=491–492}} The legend's roots go back to ''De Pylato'', a [[Latin]] prose text possibly written in the 11th century. It laid the groundwork for later works like the 12th-century ''La Destruction de Jerusalem'' and the 13th-century [[Golden Legend|Legenda Aurea]].{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=3–4}} A related narrative appears in the 14th-century [[Middle English]] alliterative poem [[Siege of Jerusalem (poem)|''Siege of Jerusalem'']], which portrays the event as "a [[Crusades|crusade]] to avenge Christ's death," as described by medieval literature scholar Christine Chism.{{Sfn|Chism|1998|p=309}} The city's fall is mentioned several times in [[Dante Alighieri|Dante]]'s ''[[Divine Comedy]]'' ({{Circa|1321}}), where the "good Titus" is portrayed as an instrument of divine retribution.{{sfn|Zissos|2016|pp=491–492}}[[File:Siege and destruction of Jerusalem (f. 155v) Cropped.jpg|thumb|upright=0.8|Medieval [[Illuminated manuscript|illumination]] of the siege and destruction of Jerusalem, from the [[Vaux Passional]] ({{circa|1504}})|alt=Medieval manuscript illumination showing knights attacking and setting fire to a walled town with red-tiled roofs and brick chimneys. Soldiers climb ladders and scale the walls, one falls headfirst from a rooftop. A partially nude, bound, bleeding figure appears near the center foreground]]
By the 17th century, artists began to adopt a more complex and ambivalent view of the event, marking a shift from earlier anti-Jewish readings.{{sfn|Zissos|2016|pp=494–495}}{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=7, 9–10}} In his ''The Destruction of the Temple at Jerusalem'' (1638), French [[Baroque painting|baroque painter]] [[Nicolas Poussin]] drew on Josephus to depict the siege as a scene of chaos and brutality. Rather than portraying Titus as a triumphant conqueror, Poussin depicted him as a conflicted figure, distressed by the ruin of the temple.{{sfn|Zissos|2016|pp=494–495}} In contrast, [[:File:Kaulbach Zerstoerung Jerusalems durch Titus.jpg|''The Destruction of Jerusalem by Titus'']] (1846) by German painter [[Wilhelm von Kaulbach]] follows the medieval Christian tradition, portraying the event as divine punishment, with avenging angels assisting Titus and the figure of the [[Wandering Jew]] being driven out by demons.{{sfn|Zissos|2016|p=493}} Another 19th-century depiction is ''Destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans'' by Scottish [[Orientalist painting|orientalist painter]] [[David Roberts (painter)|David Roberts]], who based his dramatic composition on sketches made during his 1839 visit to Jerusalem, though he took theatrical liberties with both the events and the city's topography.{{Sfn|Fine|Wisse|Joshowitz|2021|p=180}} A painting by Italian [[Romantic painter]] [[Francesco Hayez]] (1867) reflects the [[Romantic nationalism]] associated with [[Giuseppe Verdi]] and places the menorah at the center of the Temple's destruction.{{Sfn|Leone|2017|p=52}}[[File:Roberts Siege and Destruction of Jerusalem.jpg|thumb|''The Siege and Destruction of Jerusalem'', by [[David Roberts (painter)|David Roberts]] (1850). Roberts took considerable artistic liberties to create a dramatized panorama of the events|alt=19th-century panoramic painting showing Jerusalem in flames. Roman soldiers with spears and shields stand massed on a rocky outcrop in the foreground, with a commander gesturing toward the burning city. The walled city sprawls under a smoke-filled, fiery sky. Captives and fallen figures are shown in the foreground, and palm trees frame the scene at lower left]]
In [[Early modern Britain|early modern England]], the destruction of Jerusalem was seen as a mirror for national introspection.{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=7, 9–10}} Writers across genres came to identify [[Protestantism|Protestant]] England with the besieged Jews, while Catholic powers were recast as the modern Romans.{{sfn|Groves|2015|p=5}} The 1618 poem ''Canaan's Calamity'' presents the Romans as seeking "this Holy City to defile".{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=3–4}} Preacher Samuel Rolle compared Jerusalem's destruction to the [[Great Fire of London]], hinting that [[Catholic Church|Romanists]] may have been behind both.{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=3–4}} In ''[[Paradise Lost]]'' (1667), Milton presents Jerusalem's destruction as a redemptive loss, like [[Garden of Eden|Eden]]'s fall, pointing beyond judgment to spiritual renewal and the hope of the [[New Jerusalem]].{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=3–4}} This perspective fostered a more empathetic identification with Jews among the English.{{sfn|Groves|2015|pp=7, 9–10}}

In the modern era, Jerusalem's destruction has been portrayed in novels and films. In his novel ''[[Les Misérables]]'' (1862), [[Victor Hugo]] wrote that the massacre at Jerusalem diminishes Titus, and warned against the man who leaves behind a shadow in his own shape.{{sfn|Zissos|2016|p=495}} [[H. Rider Haggard|Henry Rider Haggard]]'s ''The Pearl-Maiden'' (1903) uses the fall of Jerusalem as its backdrop, telling the story of Mariam, a young Christian woman, and her love for Marcus, a Roman soldier.{{sfn|Groves|2015|p=501}} More recently, the Israeli animated film ''[[Legend of Destruction]]'' (2021), directed by [[Gidi Dar]], dramatized the siege of Jerusalem using [[Still image film|still paintings]] and a voice cast, with Talmudic figure [[Abba Sakkara|Ben Batiach]] as a central character; the film won four [[Ophir Award]]s.{{sfn|Steinberg|2023}}
