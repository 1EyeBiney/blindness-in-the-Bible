## Episode 2: Bartimaeus at Jericho

1. Bartimaeus asks to see again. That one word is the whole difference between him and the man born blind. I lost my sight in 2014, after a lifetime of seeing. I know what a face looks like, what Jericho's palms would have looked like, what a road looks like going uphill into the distance. The man born blind had nothing to compare the dark to. Bartimaeus did. Every day he sat by that road he knew exactly what he was missing.

2. He hears a crowd and has to ask what it is. I do that constantly. A room goes quiet, or everyone laughs, or chairs scrape, and I have to ask the person next to me what just happened. Most of the time people answer. Sometimes they are too busy to. Bartimaeus got an answer, and it was the most important sentence anyone ever said to him.

3. (approved by Brian) People told him to be quiet. If you are blind and loud in public, people get uncomfortable, because you are the one making a scene and they cannot make eye contact to settle you down. He shouted louder. I love that. Then, the moment Jesus noticed him, he suddenly got encouragement from the crowd when they shouted for him to take courage, he is calling you. The crowd did not change. His standing in it did. Jesus lifts everyone up.

4. (approved by Brian) He threw off his cloak. For a beggar that cloak was his bed, his coat, and the thing he spread out to collect coins. He left it in the dust because he was not planning to come back to that spot. My white cane is the thing I would never leave behind. So, for me, there would be a little pile of beat up white canes, and an iPhone. Yes, I would go back to Android.

5. (approved by Brian) Jesus asks him what he wants. He does not assume. Every blind person I know has a story about someone who grabbed an arm and steered them somewhere they did not want to go. People mean well, but often assume. Jesus had a blind man standing in front of Him and still asked. Self-advocacy at its finest.

6. Here is what gets me about Jericho. Jesus knew exactly where He was going and exactly what was waiting for Him. He had said it out loud three times. Most of us, with that ahead of us, would have put our heads down and walked. He stopped twice in one town. Once for a beggar who was shouting, and once for a little tax man up a tree that the whole city despised. Neither of them was on the way to anything. He was not teaching a crowd or healing a hundred people. He was being present for one person at a time, with the cross a few days off. That is the Jesus I want to follow, and honestly, it is the Jesus I want to be more like. I get busy and I get focused on where I am headed, and the man shouting by the road becomes an interruption. He never once treated anyone as an interruption.

7. (approved by Brian) Bartimaeus and Zacchaeus could not see Him, and for opposite reasons. One had no eyes for it. One had a crowd in the way and was too short to see over it. I know both of those. I know the first one every day. And I know the second one from every church lobby and conference hall where the crowd between me and the person I want to reach is just people, standing, talking, not unkind, just in the way. Bartimaeus shouted. Zacchaeus climbed. Both actions are not something a grown man is supposed to do in public, but hey. If they get to do it, watch out Tom and Treesa, and anyone else in the pews in front of me at church.

## Episode 3: The blind man at Bethsaida

1. (approved by Brian) People like trees walking. Of every line about blindness in the Bible, this is the one my low-vision friends can relate to most. They know exactly what he means. Shapes that move, that are the right height to be people, but with no faces and no edges. You can see something, and you cannot trust it. The Bible has one word for blindness, and most of the time it means the deep end of the pool, where I swim. This verse is the one time it describes the middle.

2. (approved by Brian) Jesus asked him how it was going. Think about that. He had put His hands on the man's eyes, and instead of announcing a result, He asked. And the man told the truth. Not yet. It is better, but not yet. Half the trouble blind people get into with well-meaning helpers is that we are too polite to say not yet. The man at Bethsaida said it to Jesus, and Jesus did not mind. He put His hands on him again.

3. (approved by Brian) Jesus led him by the hand. Not a disciple, not the friends who brought him. The same Jesus who was about to put mud on another man's eyes and send him walking across Jerusalem alone took this man by the hand and walked him out of the village Himself. Both are right. Some days you need to be sent, and some days you need a hand or an arm, and the real trick is knowing which days you need a hand and which days you don't.

4. (approved by Brian) He knew what trees looked like. That means he had seen before. Like Bartimaeus, like me, he lost it. I have noticed that the Gospels never explain how anyone lost their sight, and I have come to be grateful for that. Nobody asked him what happened. Jesus just asked whether he could see.

5. (approved by Brian) I am saying this next part while being in the middle of something, not from having reached the far shore. Something we depend on has broken this week, we cannot fix it, and we do not yet know how it gets replaced. So take what follows as a report, not as advice. My family is three disabled people, and since going blind 12 years ago, I have not watched a need go unmet. Not once. And it has even been a time of great plenty. It was only after going blind that I was given my own home and many of the comforts we enjoy.

6. (approved by Brian) Looking back, I can see God's providence as clearly as the man saw the lake at the end of this story. Looking forward, from where I am standing this week, I see only trees. That is the honest position, and I have come to think it is the one this story is actually about.

7. (approved by Brian) I may not always be able to see the path clearly, but I always know I can put my hand in His and trust Him to lead the way.

8. (approved by Brian) Let me tell you what it is actually like to take a stranger's arm. You do not know how they walk. You do not know whether they will tell you about the kerb or let you find it. Most people mean well and have no idea how to do it, and you spend the whole walk managing them, a half step behind, reading the arm for what the mouth forgot to say. Every blind person knows that walk. It is not restful. You are trusting someone who has not earned it yet, because the alternative is not moving.

9. (approved by Brian) Now think about this man. He had a town he could cross without help, and he let go of it. He let a man he had never touched take his hand and walk him out past the last house, onto ground he had never learned. And I do not think he spent that walk managing anybody. I think he knew, the way you know, that this hand was not going to let him trip. That is the part that undoes me. Not the eyes. The hand.

## Episode 4: The ones who followed

1. (approved by Brian) Two blind men following a crowd down a road and into a house. I want you to picture how that actually works, because it is not what sighted people imagine. You do not follow with your eyes, but you can hear which way the sounds are going. You follow the sound of the crowd, and you follow each other. One of them has a hand on the other's shoulder, or is holding an arm. I have done exactly this countless times when it has just been me and a blind brother or sister, and we work as a team and eventually get where we need to be. It is slower, but it works.

2. (approved by Brian) Finding the crowd was never going to be their problem. A crowd that size is the loudest thing in a town, and a house with a crowd inside it leaks noise out of every door and window. Two blind men could have walked straight to it. The problem is the last ten feet. I have stood at the edge of a packed room more times than I can count, and what I do is stop. I stand still, because if I move I bump into people, and if I bump into people I am the blind man knocking into everyone. Standing still is safe. Those two men were desperate to get to Jesus, and they could not get to Him by standing still. So they went into the crowd. That is one of the bravest things in the whole story and it gets no verse at all.

3. (approved by Brian) Jesus did not stop on the road. He let them follow Him all the way into the house. I used to think that was a little hard. Now I think He was letting them do the thing they could do, and maybe testing it. They could follow. Every step of that road was them saying we believe you are worth following, before He ever asked whether they believed. By the time He asked the question, they had already answered it with their feet.

4. (approved by Brian) Then He asked. Do you believe I am able to do this? He asked two blind men a question and waited for the answer. He did not ask the crowd about them. He did not ask whoever was with them. He did not assume that everyone knew what they wanted. He asked them. In my experience that is often half the battle.

5. (approved by Brian) The second man could not see and could not speak. Somebody had to bring him, and somebody had to speak for him, and then once he could speak, the argument started over his head and he never got a line. I notice that every time I read it. The man gets his sight and his voice, and the Pharisees immediately make it about themselves. I have been in that room. Not healed, but talked over. It is a particularly frustrating feeling.

6. (approved by Brian) I think about my friends who have lost both sight and hearing, the deaf-blind. This man in Matthew was blind and mute, which is a different pairing, but the wall is the same: you cannot take the world in, or you cannot give anything back, and either way you are cut off from people. In that world, as far as I know, there was no manual alphabet and no sign language that anyone wrote down. Helen Keller had a teacher and a system. A man like this had nothing, and the people who loved him had nothing to reach him with except a hand on his arm. I remind myself of that whenever I feel sorry for myself. My hearing is excellent, and my brain has learned to use sound in ways most sighted people never will. Blindness is a spectrum, and so is this. There were people in that crowd far worse off than me, and somebody carried one of them to Jesus.

7. According to your faith it will be done to you. I do not read that as a formula, as if the right amount of faith buys the right amount of sight. If it were, I would have been seeing years ago, and so would a lot of people I know who have more faith than I do. I read it as Jesus honoring what those two men had already done with their faith, which was follow Him down a road they could not see, into a house they had never been in, through a crowd they could not push past, to ask for something nobody could give them. He met them where their faith had already taken them.

## Episode 5: The blind in the crowd

1. (approved by Brian) Nobody on that mountain has a name. That is most of us. Most blind people in history did not get a verse of their own. They got carried up a hill by a brother or a daughter or a neighbor who decided that day was the day, and they were laid down at somebody's feet, and that was that. I think about the carriers as much as the carried. Every one of those people had someone who said, I will get you there.

2. (approved by Brian) The blind and the lame will never enter the palace. For a thousand years that saying hung around Jerusalem, and whether or not it was ever an actual rule at the Temple, people repeated it. Then Jesus clears the market out of the Temple courts, and the first people through the gap are the blind and the lame. I do not think that is an accident of timing. I think He made room and they knew it.

3. (approved by Brian) The leaders were angry about the children shouting. Matthew puts the healing and the anger in the same breath. The blind could see, the lame could walk, kids were singing, and the men in charge were indignant. When something good happens to people who are usually kept at the edges, somebody in charge is usually annoyed. That has not changed much in two thousand years.

4. (approved by Brian) Here is what I take from these two crowds. The mountain was Gentile country, and the people glorified the God of Israel. The Temple was the holiest place in the Jewish world, and the people who had been shut out came in. Blind people were in the middle of both. We were never just simply the edge of what Jesus was doing. We ... were the evidence.

5. (approved by Brian) Up a mountain. That sounds awfully scary to most blind people not named Lonnie Bedwell, who is a blind Navy veteran and climbs the kind of mountains the rest of us read about. I can tell when I have come to a place that is not usual. Up high it would feel very different. The air is cooler. The wind blows with nothing to slow it down. The sounds change, because there is nothing around you for them to bounce off. And I would get more nervous the higher we went, because I would know there was an edge somewhere and not know where.

6. (approved by Brian) So think about what it took. A blind person going up that mountain is trusting two things at once. He is trusting the person on his arm, every single step. And he is trusting that at the top there is someone worth the climb. I think what would have carried me up is the sound. If people were being healed up there for three days, it was not quiet. I would have heard shouting and laughing and crying coming down the hill toward me, and that would have done more for my nerves than anything my guide could say.

7. (approved by Brian) I keep asking myself whether this is about needing help in order to get our attention on Jesus. Every one of those people had to be brought. Not one of them could have done it alone. I did not choose blindness, but I know that needing an arm has pointed me toward Him more times than being able to manage on my own ever did. I do not say that is why any of us is blind. I only say it is what happened on that mountain, and it is what happens to me.

8. (approved by Brian) There is another thing sighted people may not think of. I have lost a lot of endurance and stamina since I went blind, because I do not get to do the physical things I used to do. A long walk on flat ground is harder for me than it was. An uneven, unpaved road is harder still. So walking up a mountain over rough ground would have been very difficult for a blind person, and that is before you remember that nobody thought to bring food. And yet if I had been given my sight up there, I sure would have hung around. I would not have missed a moment of it. It would have been a sight to behold.

9. (approved by Brian) I wrote a song recently, and one of the lines goes, I will sing before I see. It is about trusting Jesus, and the people on that mountain were doing just that. They were singing and praising without knowing where their next meal was coming from, and He fed them afterward. And I think of the joy of the companions. The people who help us are carrying our burdens too. They made that climb with somebody on their arm or on their back, and they went hungry for three days alongside us. When their friend stood up healed, they must have felt great joy too.

## Episode 6: The answer to John

1. (approved by Brian) John the Baptist doubted. The man who pointed at Jesus and said, behold the Lamb of God, sat in a dungeon and sent people to ask, are you the one, or should we wait for somebody else. For some reason, I find that enormously comforting. I think it means that having doubt while in a dark place is not a failure of faith. I think it is what faith sounds like when it is being tested, and it is allowed to ask.

2. (approved by Brian) Jesus did not answer with a sermon, or an argument, or a proclamation. He answered with people. Luke says that at that very hour He healed many, and gave sight to many who were blind, and then told John's messengers, go tell him what you have seen. When the question was, who are you, the answer began with blind people seeing. I have heard that verse so many times and it still stops me.

3. I have to be honest about the other side of it. I am a blind believer, and my eyes have not been opened. The healings in these episodes were signs, and signs point to something. They pointed John to who Jesus was. They point me there too, without my sight coming back. I would be lying if I said I never asked for it. I would also be lying if I said that is what this verse is about. This verse is not a promise that every blind person who believes will be healed in this life. It is Jesus answering a question about Himself. John asked, are You the One. And Jesus said, look at what is happening around Me, and remember what Isaiah said would happen when God came. The healings were the proof of who He is. That proof still stands, whether or not my own eyes are ever opened on this side of heaven. I do not need my sight back to know who He is. I know who He is.

4. (approved by Brian) Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me. That is the last line of the answer, and I think it was aimed at John, in prison, who was not going to be healed or rescued and knew it. Jesus was telling him: do not let what I am not doing for you make you doubt what I am. Every blind believer who has prayed for sight and gotten up the next morning still blind has heard that line.

5. (approved by Brian) The part of this episode that comes closest to home for me is not about eyes at all. It is in the verse from Isaiah that Jesus read at Nazareth. He has sent Me to bind up the brokenhearted. The work that everyone in our house does puts us straight in the path of the brokenhearted. It is less a job than a way of life, and it is far harder than most people can imagine. But I am grateful for what it has done to my heart. It keeps me humble. It teaches me to honor other people the way God wants us to, because every one of us is made in His image.

6. (approved by Brian) I know that someday I will see again. I have always known it, because I was blessed to have parents who led me to the Lord when I was very young.

7. (approved by Brian) They say blindness is the second greatest fear people have. I take no comfort in that. But I can tell you, from inside it, that blindness is not something to fear at all. What scares me is something else. It is the people who have to carry this burden without knowing the grace and mercy of our Savior, and without knowing the great joys that lie ahead for those of us who belong to Him.

## Episode 7: The stumbling block

1. (approved by Brian) Reading that first section caused me to question a few things, and the answers are now on this page, in the sections on the word and on the place and the time. It doesn't look like Hammurabi's code has anything about the treatment of the blind. So is the Bible the first recorded anything that addresses the blind? And when was Hammurabi's code written compared with what Moses was doing in Leviticus and Deuteronomy?

2. (approved by Brian) Then the stumbling block itself. For me, a stumbling block is an annoyance more than anything: something I would stumble over, but not necessarily fall down. I stumble over curbs and parking stops all the time and somehow always seem to catch myself. I am fortunate to have enough of a sense of balance and spatial awareness for this to work. Many blind people I know will easily trip over curbs and parking stops, usually resulting in serious pain of some sort. So is this notion of a stumbling block more like me tripping slightly, or more like a blind person falling down and seriously hurting something?

3. (approved by Brian) When I started studying this episode, my first thought was that this law did not mean much to me, because of what I took a stumbling block to be: something you trip over and catch yourself. But the further I read, the more I found comfort where I had not expected it. I feel the gravity of what God was telling His people so long ago. He is always watching, even when nobody else is, and He knows.

## Episode 8: The servant's eye

1. (approved by Brian) Reading this law, the first thing that struck me was the pairing of an eye with a tooth. I have lost two teeth since I lost my two eyes, and the adjustment was not remotely the same. Put plainly: a servant with a loose tooth could be struck once and walk out free, and the law sets that beside a man who will never see again. It seemed unbalanced. Then I read it as the old commentators did. The tooth is not there because it equals an eye. It is there because it is the smallest lasting harm anyone would bother to name. If even that freed a servant, then nothing a master could do to a servant's body was free of cost. The eye was never in question.

2. (approved by Brian) The second thing was the money. The tables from Babylon we just heard put silver on an eye, less silver on a tooth, and for a servant's eye, a payment to the owner. How do you price reduced sight, or no sight at all? I would guess that some of these servants were left completely blind. Is that worth the price of freedom? The Law of Moses does not try to answer in silver. It names no sum. It gives the servant the one thing the master cannot keep back, which is himself.

3. (approved by Brian) Here is the other side of it, and I mean this. I have lived without light since 2014. My freedom, and above all my freedom to worship God in the way I choose, is worth more to me than my sight ever was. I have adapted, adjusted, and found God's purpose for me. In so many ways my blindness has been a gift from God, the third greatest after my salvation and my wife. It has brought us more blessings than I can list, and it has shaped my character to fit His purposes better than it did.

4. (approved by Brian) So I read this law two ways at once. Freedom is not a price for an eye, and the law does not pretend it is. But freedom is not a small thing to hand a blind man, either.

5. (approved by Brian) I have never been a slave, and most blind people I know never will be. But most of us have felt the shackles of blindness all the same. In the early days it is a kind of prison. There are rooms you cannot cross without help, and a world that stops where your hands can reach. Other people decide where you go and when. I felt those bindings. So when this law hands a blinded servant his freedom, I do not need to have been a servant to understand what he was given. I understand freedom, because I know what it is to lose it, and to be given it back.

6. (approved by Brian) That is how the gift I spoke of came to me. It did not come all at once, and it did not come by getting my sight back. It came by being set free inside the dark. The servant in this law walked out the door the day he lost his eye. It took me longer. But I walked out too.

## Episode 9: The blind priest

1. (approved by Brian) Reading this law, I had three questions, and the section on the place and the time now answers them. I wanted to know what a priest actually did, and it turns out that most of it was done with the hands: the fire, the lamp, the sacrifices, the blood. I wondered whether a blind priest would touch things he should not, since I touch things all day to know what they are, and I learned that the rules about touching holy things were for the Levites who carried them, not for the priests who handled them. And I asked whether fitting a blind priest in would have burdened the rest, and the numbers say no. Twenty-four courses, a week each, fifty men on duty at a time. One blind man among them would hardly have been noticed.

2. (approved by Brian) I find it hard to weigh this law as if it were mine to keep. We live on the far side of the cross. Our worship is not built around one altar and one family of priests, and the sacrifice this law guarded has been made once and for all. The word section above says it better than I can: the word that kept the blind priest from the altar is the word for the lamb that was offered on it. So I read this law as what it was, a rule for a few hundred men and one duty, and I do not read it as a word about what blind people are worth. The text itself takes care of that. He stayed a priest, and he ate at the table.

3. (approved by Brian) What I can speak to is now. I know quite a few blind pastors and spiritual leaders, and I am grateful beyond words that they are allowed to minister and to do God's work with the talents and abilities they have. Nobody keeps them from the pulpit for a defect. I am glad we do not have that requirement.

4. (approved by Brian) But we do have something else, and in some ways it is more shackling, because nobody wrote it down. It is low expectations. A rule can be read and argued with. An expectation just sits there, in the way people hesitate before handing you a task, or thank you too much for doing an ordinary thing. The old rabbis, arguing about whether a one-eyed priest could give the blessing, worried that the people would stare. One of them allowed it if the town was used to him. That is still the whole question. Not what the blind man can do, but what the people around him expect.

5. (approved by Brian) So the blind priest of Leviticus and the blind pastor I know are in the same place after all. Both are kept, or freed, less by a law than by what the people in front of them are used to.

6. (approved by Brian) I need to know more about priestly duties. Would a blind priest accidentally touch things he should not? I have to touch things all the time to figure out what they are; would that factor in? And would trying to let the blind priest fit in have been a hindrance to the rest of the Levites?

## Episode 10: The blind animal and the altar

1. (approved by Brian) This one was harder to read than I expected, and not because of what it asks of a lamb. It is because it puts blindness straight into the category of broken. In society's terms I am disabled. But when I hear of a disabled car, I can swap in the word broken without losing anything, and I most certainly do not feel broken. I do most tasks differently now, with a different set of skills and more steps. Yet I still carry nearly all the same obligations, responsibilities, enjoyments and pleasures that I had with full sight.

2. (approved by Brian) If anything, I am better at the things God wants me to be better at. Fewer distractions means more focused attention on the people I love, and on the people I need to go and find to love.

3. (approved by Brian) The law itself leaves me a door. The blind firstborn was not thrown away. Deuteronomy says to eat it at home, as you would a gazelle or a deer, and Matthew Henry says what that means: not fit for the altar is not the same as worthless. A thing with a defect still fed a family. I would not have chosen the word defect for myself. But I notice that even this law, the hardest one in this series for me to read, does not call the blind animal broken. It calls it ordinary.
